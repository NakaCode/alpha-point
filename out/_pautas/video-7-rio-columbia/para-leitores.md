# A video about the Columbia River: material for a cold read

## A · Three titles

1. Why the U.S. Pays Canada to Keep Part of a Lake Empty
2. Why the U.S. Pays Canada for Empty Space in a Lake
3. Why the U.S. Pays Canada for Empty Space on the Columbia River

## B · What the video tells

The Columbia River starts in British Columbia and flows into the United States. About 15 percent of its basin is in Canada, but by US
agencies' count that part supplies about 38 percent of the average flow at The Dalles, Oregon. Under a 1964 treaty, Canada built three storage
dams. The US prepaid 60 years of flood protection, and it gives Canada, as electricity, half of the extra power its dams make from that
storage. That protection ended in September 2024. Since the spring floods of 2025, under a temporary deal, the US has paid Canada
37.6 million dollars a year, adjusted for inflation, to keep space empty in one Canadian reservoir. The treaty remains in force, and talks on a
new version have been paused since March 2025. A 2012 US estimate said ending the treaty would raise US power revenue by 180 to 280 million
dollars a year. British Columbia disputed it, and estimated that relying only on on-call flood help would cost the US 40 to 150 million dollars a
year in lost power.

## D · Two openings (as the narrator would say them)

**Opening 1.** *(The first image is the lake, low, from the air.)*

Every spring, before the floods, part of the Arrow Lakes Reservoir, in British Columbia, is emptied on purpose. And the United States pays Canada
for that empty space: 37.6 million dollars a year, adjusted for inflation. It's a temporary deal, and it took effect for the spring floods of 2025.
Only about 15 percent of the Columbia River's basin lies in Canada. Yet look at a river gauge in British Columbia, about a dozen miles north of the
border. The Columbia there already carries about 39 percent of the water that passes The Dalles, in Oregon. That's the average of 88 years. The deal
runs out after the spring of 2027, unless the two countries agree on something new. And talks on a new treaty have been on pause since March 2025.
Here's the strange part. Even without this deal, the treaty still lets the United States call on Canada for help in a big flood. So why does the
United States pay for empty space, when it can already call on Canada in a flood? You're at Alpha Point, and this is where the story of the lake
that waits for the flood begins.

**Opening 2.** *(The first image is the river, from the air, in British Columbia.)*

Before the big storage dams went in upstream, the Columbia River here, in British Columbia, carried about ten times more water in June than in
February. Since 1974, its busiest month has carried only about twice its quietest. And over a whole year, the total has barely changed. The water
wasn't taken away. The big dams upstream, on both sides of the border, moved it: to make power in winter, and to leave room for the spring flood.
The three in Canada, built under a 1964 treaty, can store as much water as the whole river carries past The Dalles, in Oregon, in about six weeks.
And every spring, before the floods, part of one of those lakes, the Arrow Lakes Reservoir, is emptied on purpose. The United States pays Canada
for that empty space: 37.6 million dollars a year, under a temporary deal that took effect for the spring floods of 2025. That deal runs out after
the spring of 2027, unless the two countries agree on something new. And even without it, the treaty still lets the United States call on Canada
for help in a big flood. So why does the United States pay for empty space, when it can already call on Canada in a flood? You're at Alpha Point,
and this is where the story of a river two countries reshaped begins.
