## B · What the video tells

North of the Grand Canyon is a part of Arizona with a little more land than New Jersey (about 7,850 square miles) and fewer than 10,000 people. The
canyon separates it from the rest of the state: the two rims are about 10 miles apart, but about 212 miles apart by road, and below the Navajo Bridge no
road crosses the river for about 346 miles. From Colorado City, the largest town up there, the fastest highway route to the county seat, Kingman, is
about 266 miles, about 166 of them in Utah and Nevada; staying inside Arizona, it is about 376. The shortest highway route to Kingman also leaves Arizona from
every town in the part of the area that belongs to Mohave County, home to about 80 percent of its people. The east side, around Fredonia, belongs to another
county and reaches its county seat without leaving Arizona. The state line up there is not the canyon but a straight line, the 37th parallel, set by
Congress in 1850, when the area was part of New Mexico Territory and the canyon was barely on any map. The laws give no reason for the choice. When
Congress created Arizona in 1863, it kept the line. In 1866, when it gave Arizona's northwest corner to Nevada, Congress did make the river a state line,
below the canyon. The land north of the canyon was settled mostly from Utah. Utah asked Congress for it in 1865, for a port on the river, and again and
again from 1897 to 1919, arguing that geographically it belonged to Utah. In 1905, residents petitioned Congress to join Utah, writing that they were 250
miles from their county seat, while thanking Arizona for its courtesies. Arizona opposed every request on record. In 1903, its territorial legislature told
Congress that the land was rich in minerals, timber and grazing land, "of inestimable value" as a source of revenue, and that the Grand Canyon was
"peculiarly and exclusively an Arizona endowment"; Arizona, it said, was "unalterably opposed." Congress never acted on Utah's requests. In 1912, Arizona
became a state. In 1919, Utah's commissioners went to Phoenix, found "overwhelming opposition," and, according to a Utah newspaper, one of them was assured that a
road from St. George, Utah, to Kingman, with a bridge over the Colorado near the canyon's western end, would be built. No road crosses the river there
today; a ferry ran there on and off until the rising water of Lake Mead covered the landing in 1938. Today, the federal office that manages much of the
area's land is in St. George, Utah; for more than four decades, the ambulance for Fredonia came from Kanab, Utah; and Colorado City, Arizona, and Hildale,
Utah, one community split by the state line, are an hour apart for 238 days a year.

**Questions:** Is each title honest, exaggerated or misleading, given these facts? Is the video fair to both Utah and Arizona? What did you miss?

## D · Two openings (as the narrator would say them)

**Opening 1.** *(The first image is an aerial shot flying across the Grand Canyon, with the far rim in view.)*

The North Rim and the South Rim of the Grand Canyon are about ten miles apart. By road, they're about 212. And the land on the north side, all the way up
to Utah, is still Arizona. Up there is a town called Colorado City. The fastest highway route from Colorado City to the seat of its own county government,
Kingman, is more than 260 miles. More than 160 of those miles are in Utah and Nevada. Staying inside Arizona, it's about 375. For decades, Utah asked for
this land. Arizona said no. The last request we found was in 1919. And Arizona's border up here isn't the canyon. It's a straight line. So who drew a
straight line across canyon country, and why did Arizona fight to keep the land on the other side? You're at [channel name], and this is where the story
of the land beyond the canyon begins.

**Opening 2.** *(The first image is the high plateau north of the canyon, from the air.)*

In December 1905, people living north of the Grand Canyon signed a petition to Congress. They lived in Arizona, and they asked to be annexed to Utah. "We
are 250 miles from our county seat," they wrote. That land is still Arizona: everything between the canyon and the Utah line. And from Colorado City, the
largest town up there, the fastest highway route to its county seat, Kingman, is more than 260 miles. More than 160 of them are in Utah and Nevada. Utah
kept asking for this land. The last request we found was in 1919. Arizona said no. And Arizona's border up here isn't the canyon. It's a straight line. So
who drew a straight line across canyon country, and why did Arizona fight to keep the land on the other side? You're at [channel name], and this is where
