## B · What the video tells

Australia is famous for venomous animals. One of its snakes, the inland taipan, lives on floodplains in the outback and has the most potent venom of any
land snake, as measured on lab mice in a 1979 study; by 2017, doctors had documented only about a dozen people envenomed by one, three of them by snakes in
the wild. According to one biologist, about 145 of Australia's roughly 220 snake species are venomous, while about 15 percent of the world's snakes are.
Another researcher says that, apart from the snakes, Australia's venomous animals are "not unusual at all for a largely tropical area," and that the
country's image as the home of highly venomous animals is, "for the most part," overstated. Australia's official death records show that from 2016 to 2025,
all venomous animals combined killed 85 people: 35 by venomous snakes, 42 by bees and wasps, and none by spiders, although spider bites put Australians in
the hospital about 12,000 times in a little over a decade. That works out to about a one-in-three-million chance per year; road crashes killed more people
in an average month of 2025. The video explains where the snakes came from: Australia broke away from Antarctica 80 to 85 million years ago and drifted
north, and a family of front-fanged venomous snakes, the elapids, arrived from Asia roughly 25 to 30 million years ago. They seem to have been the first
venomous snakes to reach the continent, which had no vipers, and today they fill most of the roles snakes play on land. The video also explains why so
few people die. The inland taipan is "usually quite shy," in the words of a state government fact sheet. The brown snake, which shares a toxin with it
that no other snakes have, caused 17 of the 23 snakebite deaths in one national study from 2005 to 2015, and the video compares where each snake lives with
where people live. Funnel-web spiders killed at least 13 people before an antivenom came into use in 1981, and no death has been confirmed since. Bees and
wasps kill mostly through severe allergic reactions. Animals without venom (dogs, cattle and horses, sea animals, crocodiles) killed 154 people over the
same decade; extreme heat killed 202, and drowning in rivers, lakes and the sea more than 1,000. Some of the explanations are the researchers' hypotheses,
and the video says so. It ends at a reptile park near Sydney that milks funnel-web spiders for antivenom and asks people to catch the spiders they find and
bring them in.

## D · Two openings (as the narrator would say them)

**Opening 1.** *(The first image is an aerial shot of cracked-clay floodplains and winding river channels in the Australian outback.)*

Out on the floodplains of the Australian outback lives the land snake with the most potent venom on Earth, as measured on lab mice. And it has plenty of
company. Of Australia's roughly 220 kinds of snakes, about 145 are venomous, a biologist told Live Science. Worldwide, only about 15 percent of snakes are.
Now look at the country's official death records. From 2016 to 2025, every venomous animal in Australia combined killed 85 people. Spiders killed none.
That's fewer than Australia's roads killed in an average month of 2025. Measured against the population, that's about a one-in-three-million chance each
year. So why are so many of Australia's snakes venomous, and why do so few people die? You're at [channel name], and this is where the story of Australia's
venom begins.

**Opening 2.** *(The first image is a close-up of a funnel-web spider; then Sydney from the air.)*

Australia's funnel-web spiders killed at least 13 people before 1981. That year, an antivenom came into use. Since then, doctors haven't confirmed a single
death. In the country's official death records, from 2016 to 2025, spiders killed no one at all. Every venomous animal in Australia together killed 85
people. That's fewer than Australia's roads killed in an average month of 2025. And yet, of Australia's roughly 220 kinds of snakes, about 145 are
venomous, a biologist told Live Science. Worldwide, only about 15 percent of snakes are. So why are so many of Australia's snakes venomous, and why do so
few people die? You're at [channel name], and this is where the story of Australia's venom begins.
