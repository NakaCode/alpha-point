## Video 1 · B · What the video tells

Los Angeles was founded inland, about nineteen miles from the shallow harbor at Wilmington, on a coast with no natural deep-water port. Army engineers
first deepened the shallow estuary with a jetty, and after a long fight between a railroad pier at Santa Monica and supporters of San Pedro Bay, a
federal board chose San Pedro for the deep-water harbor in 1897. By 1906, that harbor sat inside two separate cities, San Pedro and Wilmington, and
California law let cities merge only if their borders touched. That year, Los Angeles annexed a strip about half a mile wide and roughly fifteen miles
long, running south to the edge of those two towns but stopping short of the water. In 1909, voters in all three cities approved consolidation, and Los
Angeles had a coastline of its own for the first time; today about nine miles of that strip still run thin between neighboring cities.

## Video 2 · B · What the video tells

Under a 1903 treaty, the United States was granted "in perpetuity" a band about ten miles wide across Panama, from the Caribbean to the Pacific, with the
powers it would have "if it were the sovereign"; that Canal Zone got its own government, with a governor appointed by the U.S. president, a federal court
whose juries were drawn from U.S. citizens, and its own police, schools and post office. The video opens in Balboa, the town below the Zone's
headquarters, where about half of the 3,139 residents in 1960 had been born in the United States, while other Zone towns such as Paraíso and Rainbow City
had almost none. It explains that being born in the Zone did not by itself make anyone a U.S. citizen (a 1937 law tied it to the parents), and that from
1939 only people who worked or served there, and their families, could live inside the line. For decades the Zone's workers were also sorted into a "gold
roll," mostly U.S. citizens, and a "silver roll," mostly West Indian and Panamanian workers, names left over from construction-era pay in gold and silver
coin; the labels ran through pay, housing, the company store and the clubhouses until 1948, alongside "white" and "colored" schools, and the division
outlived the names. The video then covers four days of fighting that began over a flag at Balboa High School in January 1964, and the 1977 treaty under
which the Zone and its government ceased to exist on October 1, 1979, while noting that American police and courts kept covering U.S. employees in some
areas for 30 more months and that a U.S. agency ran the canal until the end of 1999.
