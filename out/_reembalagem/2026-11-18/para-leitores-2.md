## Video 1 · B · What the video tells

A line running along the Andes, the continental divide, splits Peru in two: about three-quarters of the country drains east toward the Amazon and holds
more than 97 percent of Peru's water, while the Pacific side, whose coast is mostly desert, has about 2 percent. Yet that dry side is where most Peruvians
live, including the capital, Lima, which with its port, Callao, is home to more than ten million people and gets about a third of an inch of rain a year.
The video explains why that side is so dry (a cold ocean and a lid of sinking air over the Pacific) and why people settled there anyway: green river
valleys where a city rose about 5,000 years ago, a Spanish capital founded near a very good port in 1535, and a 20th-century migration down from the
highlands, pushed in part by a shortage of farmland and later by political violence. It then follows Lima's water: more than half of what the city
stores for the dry season sits in high lakes on the side that drains to the Amazon and crosses the divide through a single tunnel, in service since 1963,
while more than 700,000 people in Lima and Callao have no connection to the water network and many buy water from tanker trucks. It ends with the risk of
depending on that one tunnel in a major earthquake, since a second tunnel, planned since the 1990s, still had no construction date as of late August 2026.

## Video 2 · B · What the video tells

This video is about the deepest point in the Atlantic Ocean: a spot more than five miles down (about 27,500 feet), in an undersea trench about 83 miles
off the coast of Puerto Rico, a U.S. island. It opens with two comparisons: if Mount Everest stood on that spot, its top 1,500 feet or so would stick out
of the water, and the Mariana Trench, the place most people think of as the deepest ocean, is in the Pacific, about 9,500 miles away. From there it
follows how people measured and explored the trench, from a weighted wire lowered in 1883 and a Navy cruiser's sonar reading in 1939 to the first person
to reach the deepest point, an explorer who went down alone in his own submersible in December 2018. Along the way it covers why the trench is so deep,
the old landslides and tsunamis around it, a rival trench in the far South Atlantic that turned out about 370 feet shallower, and a 2019 study showing
that the official list of undersea features still had the Atlantic more than 1,000 feet too deep. One thread runs from the start to the end: in 1964 the
crew of a French deep-diving craft reported counting about 200 fish some 24,000 feet down in the trench; in 2019 a submersible went back to the same
spot, saw no fish of that kind but found sea cucumbers spread about as thickly and similar in look, and a 2020 paper concluded that the 1964 "fish" were
sea cucumbers, a conclusion drawn from the count and the look, since the animals seen that day can't be checked again.
