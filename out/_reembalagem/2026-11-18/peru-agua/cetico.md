**N1 · Why Peru's People and Water Are on Opposite Sides of the Andes** (62)
- **Paga:** F2–F4, aos 16,9 s: "East of it, about three-quarters of the country drains toward the Amazon and holds more than 97 percent of
  Peru's water. West of it, toward the Pacific, is the dry side, with about 2 percent. But that is where most Peruvians live." O "why" é o
  vídeo inteiro, e o fecho o devolve: "Not because that side has the water. First, the valleys… Then the port… Then the people…"
- **Por quê:** é o paradoxo inteiro numa imagem (a gente de um lado, a água do outro), que faltava ao L1, com "Peru" e "Andes" para a busca.
- **Cuidado:** "Peru's People" é figura, como o "Peru Lives" do L1: um terço mora do lado da Amazônia (33,32 % no PNRH).

**N2 · Why Lima Keeps Water on the Other Side of the Andes** (51)
- **Paga:** F9, aos 39,2 s: "And more than half of the water Lima stores for the dry season sits in lakes on the other side of the line." E no
  p5 (7:24): "So Lima's water utility holds back water from the wet months in lakes and reservoirs high in the Andes"; no p7: "The water Lima
  keeps on the far side of those mountains has only one way through."
- **Por quê:** uma imagem estranha (uma cidade que guarda água do outro lado de uma cordilheira), como o "Keep Part of a Lake Empty" do Columbia.
  Sem "Its": não diz que toda a água está lá.
- **Cuidado:** o "why" se paga em parte. O vídeo dá a necessidade (o Rímac baixo por sete meses, a cidade de 10 milhões) e o propósito do túnel
  (a usina e o rio de Lima), não um motivo para guardar justamente do outro lado. E o tema do vídeo (por que a gente mora no lado seco) fica
  fora do título.

**N3 · Lima Barely Gets Rain. How Do 10 Million People Get Water?** (58)

## 5 · Para os leitores

A line running along the Andes, the continental divide, splits Peru in two: about three-quarters of the country drains east toward the
Amazon and holds more than 97 percent of Peru's water, while the Pacific side, whose coast is mostly desert, has about 2 percent. Yet that dry
side is where most Peruvians live, including the capital, Lima, which with its port, Callao, is home to more than ten million people and gets
about a third of an inch of rain a year. The video explains why that side is so dry (a cold ocean and a lid of sinking air over the Pacific)
and why people settled there anyway: green river valleys where a city rose about 5,000 years ago, a Spanish capital founded near a very good
port in 1535, and a 20th-century migration down from the highlands, pushed in part by a shortage of farmland and later by political violence.
It then follows Lima's water: more than half of what the city stores for the dry season sits in high lakes on the side that drains to the
Amazon and crosses the divide through a single tunnel, in service since 1963, while more than 700,000 people in Lima and Callao have no
connection to the water network and many buy water from tanker trucks. It ends with the risk of depending on that one tunnel in a major
earthquake, since a second tunnel, planned since the 1990s, still had no construction date as of late August 2026.
