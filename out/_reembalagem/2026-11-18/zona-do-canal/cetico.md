## 5. Para os leitores

> Under a 1903 treaty, the United States was granted "in perpetuity" a band about ten miles wide across Panama, from the Caribbean to the
> Pacific, with the powers it would have "if it were the sovereign"; that Canal Zone got its own government, with a governor appointed by the
> U.S. president, a federal court whose juries were drawn from U.S. citizens, and its own police, schools and post office. The video opens in
> Balboa, the town below the Zone's headquarters, where about half of the 3,139 residents in 1960 had been born in the United States, while
> other Zone towns such as Paraíso and Rainbow City had almost none. It explains that being born in the Zone did not by itself make anyone a
> U.S. citizen (a 1937 law tied it to the parents), and that from 1939 only people who worked or served there, and their families, could live
> inside the line. For decades the Zone's workers were also sorted into a "gold roll," mostly U.S. citizens, and a "silver roll," mostly West
> Indian and Panamanian workers, names left over from construction-era pay in gold and silver coin; the labels ran through pay, housing, the
> company store and the clubhouses until 1948, alongside "white" and "colored" schools, and the division outlived the names. The video then
> covers four days of fighting that began over a flag at Balboa High School in January 1964, and the 1977 treaty under which the Zone and its
> government ceased to exist on October 1, 1979, while noting that American police and courts kept covering U.S. employees in some areas for
> 30 more months and that a U.S. agency ran the canal until the end of 1999.
