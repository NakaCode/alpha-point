On August twenty-seventh, nineteen seventy-four, Alaska voters approved moving their state capital out of Juneau. Half a century later, the capital is still in Juneau. So what happened between that vote and the move that never came? The answer comes down to one line that showed up on later ballots: the price.

You can't drive to Juneau from the rest of the continent. Its roads run about forty-three miles north, to Cascade Point, and stop. Every car, passenger and load of freight comes and goes by air or by sea.

For the people who wanted the capital out, that was the point. One of them, Harris, chaired a committee pushing the move. He made the case in the official voter pamphlet of nineteen eighty-two. Travel there, he wrote, "is so expensive and time-consuming that few ordinary citizens are able to go there."

Juneau got the capital early, and the rest of Alaska grew up somewhere else. The city began as a gold camp.

The capital came in stages. In nineteen hundred, a federal law named Juneau the temporary seat of government, but the government stayed in Sitka, the old Russian capital. The governor's office moved to Juneau in nineteen oh-six. And the nineteen twelve law creating the Territory of Alaska was plain: "the capital of the Territory of Alaska shall be at the city of Juneau, Alaska."

Alaska's population, though, moved north. A railroad work camp became Anchorage. Around World War Two, military bases went up at Anchorage and Fairbanks. The Alaska and Glenn highways tied Anchorage to the continent's roads. The state labor department says the Anchorage and Fairbanks areas held roughly fifteen percent of Alaskans in nineteen forty. By nineteen sixty, they held more than half.

Voters rejected moving the capital in nineteen sixty and nineteen sixty-two. In August nineteen seventy-four, they approved it, through a ballot initiative, a law that voters themselves put on the ballot. Anchorage's districts voted eighty percent yes. The Southeast, around Juneau, voted about ninety-four percent no. Fairbanks split nearly even.

The nineteen seventy-four law also set a rule: up to three candidate sites, none within thirty miles of Anchorage or Fairbanks. The state Supreme Court upheld the initiative that same year. As the attorney general summed it up in nineteen ninety-three, the premise was a planned capital away from the big cities.

That nineteen seventy-four vote decided the capital would leave. What remained was choosing the place, and saying what it would cost.

In November nineteen seventy-six, voters picked the place. The ballot offered three sites. The winner, with a little more than half the vote, lay near the town of Willow, in the Mat-Sue Valley. The site was about seventy road miles north of Anchorage, and five miles east of Willow itself.

In Fairbanks, a group wanted to know the price first. In early nineteen seventy-seven, they organized the Frank committee, its name short for "Frustrated, Responsible Alaskans Needing Knowledge." Their initiative said the state could not spend money on the move until voters approved bonds covering its costs through nineteen ninety-two. A bond is money the state borrows and pays back over many years.

The move's supporters answered the Frank initiative in the official nineteen seventy-eight voter pamphlet. "Its true purpose is to prohibit the move to Willow," their statement said.

Then came the price. In November nineteen seventy-eight, the Frank initiative went to the voters. On the same ballot sat the money to build: up to nine hundred sixty-six million dollars in bonds for the new capital, almost a billion dollars. The same voters made two choices. They passed the Frank initiative, with nearly fifty-six percent. And they rejected the bonds, by almost three to one. Willow had a site, and no money approved to build on it.

In nineteen eighty-one, the legislature rewrote the rules. There would be no bond vote this time. Instead, voters would get one last question in November nineteen eighty-two: could the state spend the money the move would take? The ballot printed the state's estimate: about two point eight billion dollars, counted through nineteen ninety-four. The estimate included nearly five hundred ninety million dollars to compensate Juneau residents for the move.

It was winner take all. A yes vote meant building at Willow. A no vote meant Juneau stayed the capital.

Harris, the committee chair quoted earlier, called the figures inflated. Jim Clark, against the move, reminded voters of the bonds they turned down in nineteen seventy-eight.

The pro-move side had a mascot, Access Annie. Lee Stoops, a legislative aide who lived in Juneau, answered with a rival: Football Fannie. In twenty twenty-six, he told Anchorage Daily News columnist David Reamer why. "I decided to just make fun of the whole process and the amount of money they wanted to spend to move the capital and build in Willow." Fannie's first ad priced twenty-eight N F L teams and three domed stadiums at eight hundred million dollars. The ad called that about a quarter of the Willow price tag.

A Dittman Research poll in late September had the move ahead, fifty-two percent to forty-five. In November nineteen eighty-two, the no side won, with nearly fifty-three percent.

Why voters said no is still argued. What they said no to is not. It was a printed price. Stoops says the satire decided it. Reamer calls it one factor, at the very least. Before the vote, pollster Dave Dittman didn't think the price tag weighed that heavily. In nineteen ninety, state labor economist Brian Rae wrote that the expense "finally persuaded voters to reject the plans." Reamer adds that opponents spent more than a million dollars.

By the ballot's own terms, the nineteen eighty-two no vote also repealed every move law, from the nineteen seventy-four initiative to the Frank initiative.

The move came back in nineteen ninety-four. The new site was Wasilla, another Mat-Sue Valley town, and the ballot carried no price. In the official election pamphlet, supporters said Wasilla was on the road system. Opponents recalled the nineteen eighty-two estimate of about two point eight billion dollars. "This time," the statement against it said, "no price tag is attached."

In November nineteen ninety-four, nearly fifty-five percent voted no on Wasilla. It lost without a price tag, so the price can't explain every defeat. The same ballot carried a second initiative: the right to know the cost first. It passed with about seventy-seven percent.

That initiative revived the nineteen seventy-eight idea, and its wording is still law. State money can move the capital or the legislature only after voters approve bonds for twelve years of costs. The rule covers the legislature too: lawmakers could move their sessions even with the capital still in Juneau. The law calls for a nine-member commission to tally the costs, including social, economic and environmental ones. The stated purpose is the people's "right to know."

Eight years later, voters rejected moving the legislative sessions to the Mat-Sue Valley, by about two to one. That measure would also have dropped the cost rule for moving the legislature.

In twenty twenty-five, a House bill to hold legislative sessions in Anchorage proposed repealing the whole cost law, for the capital as well as the legislature. As of September twenty twenty-six, the bill hadn't left its first committee. Juneau's state senator, Jesse Keel, told Kay-too in twenty twenty-five: "It's a little bit like a zombie movie. It's never all the way dead."

Then there's the road. If getting to Juneau is the complaint, why not just build one? When a federal project could significantly change the environment, federal law requires an environmental impact statement, a study of what the project would change. The twenty eighteen study for a Juneau road says travel limits feed the perception of an isolated capital. Capital move supporters, it adds, often cite that.

Look at a map and you'll see the problem with a road. Behind Juneau, an icefield straddles the border with British Columbia, the Canadian province next door. That leaves two corridors: the Taku River valley to the east, and Lynn Canal to the north. British Columbia said in nineteen ninety-three and two thousand three it wasn't interested in the Taku. Lynn Canal is a long fjord between steep mountains, not a dug canal.

Any road north would have to reach Haines or Skagway, at the top of that fjord. From there, the roads out cross into Canada and meet the Alaska Highway in the Yukon. With no such road, Juneau's official highway link is the state ferry system, called the Alaska Marine Highway.

In January two thousand, Governor Tony Knowles picked the state's preferred option: a road up the east side of Lynn Canal. Because of its high cost, he said, he wouldn't actively pursue it while he was in office. That October, Juneau voters split almost evenly. Better ferries finished just seventy-nine votes ahead of a road.

Highway officials chose a version of that route in two thousand six. It stopped short of Haines and Skagway, so drivers would finish the trip on a ferry. The study counts forty-one avalanche paths crossing it, and puts its cost at about six hundred eighty million dollars. In two thousand nine, a federal court threw out that choice. The state first had to weigh better service with its existing ferries.

Then the money ran short. The study notes that for a decade, oil supplied about ninety percent of the state's unrestricted general fund revenue. That's the money that runs state government. From a high in twenty thirteen to a low in early twenty sixteen, oil prices fell by more than three quarters. In December twenty sixteen, Governor Bill Walker said he preferred what the study calls the No Action alternative: keep the ferries and build no road. He cited the state's fiscal crisis.

The Juneau Assembly, the city council, still backed the road in January twenty seventeen, six to three. Assembly member Maria Gladyshevsky voted no. "It would be a lot harder for me," she said, "if it were actually a road to Skagway or a road to Haines but it's a road to another ferry."

The No Action alternative became final in June twenty eighteen.

In the end, the road ran into a price too. The twenty eighteen decision pointed to the state's budget, and to a public fight over the road, a fight that was never settled. The road north was never built, and Juneau's highway still stops about forty-three miles north, at Cascade Point.

So what happened to that yes vote in nineteen seventy-four? The question changed. In nineteen seventy-four, the question was whether to move. Voters said yes. Then it became how much. Both times a price went on the ballot, voters said no, though why is still debated. Since nineteen ninety-four, the law asks how much first. For the capital to leave, voters would have to approve its price before state money could move it.

That yes vote was real. It just never came with a price voters would approve.
