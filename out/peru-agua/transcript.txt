A line runs along the Andes and divides Peru. East of it, about three-quarters of the country drains toward the Amazon and holds more than 97% of Peru's water. West of it, toward the Pacific, is the dry side, with about 2%. But that is where most Peruvians live. In 2012, by the National Water Plan's figures, more than three in five did. The coast on that side is mostly desert. There, the capital, Lima, gets about a third of an inch of rain a year. With its port, Callao, it is home to more than 10 million people. And more than half of the water Lima stores for the dry season sits in lakes on the other side of the line. That water reaches the city through a single tunnel, built in the early 1960s. So why do most Peruvians live on the dry side of that line? You're at Alpha Point, and this is where the story of Peru's water divide begins. That line is the Continental Divide. Rain that falls on its western side drains to the Pacific. Rain on its eastern side runs into rivers that reach the Amazon and then the Atlantic. A small part of the south drains into Lake Titicaca instead. In Peru, the divide runs along the western range of the Andes, through the Lima region itself. From downtown Lima, it is less than 70 miles away. On the dry side, dozens of short, steep rivers drop from the mountains to the sea. Lima's own river, the Rimac, is one of them. On the other side, the rivers are long, and they gather into the Amazon. There, Loreto, Peru's largest region, is also one of its most thinly populated. Its capital, Iquitos, can only be reached by air or river, as Harvard's growth lab puts it. Peru's national water plan puts the two sides in a single table. The dry side covers less than a quarter of the country. Per person, the Amazon side has about a hundred times as much water. The country's water authority calls that imbalance a paradox. Peru's weather service says the main reason the coast is so dry lies out over the Pacific. Over the South Pacific sits a vast high-pressure system, and under it, the air sinks. Off the shore, three things keep the sea unusually cold for its latitude. The cold Humboldt current, water rising from the deep, and a blanket of low cloud. The cold sea chills the air above it, and the sinking air settles on top as a lid of warmer air. Under that lid, the moisture cannot rise to build a rain cloud. It stays as fog, low cloud, and drizzle. You can see the system by what happens when it weakens. In El Nino years, the high pressure and the trade winds ease, and the cold water slows or stops rising. The sea warms, and the desert coast gets heavy rain and floods, mostly in the north. In a normal year, Lima gets about a third of an inch of rain, almost all of it drizzle. The same weather service calls Lima one of the largest cities in the world located in a desert. The mountains help. They block moist air from the Amazon up to about 16,000 feet. On the eastern slopes, that air rises, and its moisture falls as rain. East of the Andes, the town of Satipo gets more than 6 feet of rain a year. The dry side gets real rain too, but mostly high up, and most of it from December to March. That rain runs off fast, down short rivers. Some of these rivers run dry for part of the year. In 2009, Peru's water authority said that, for lack of storage, about half of the floodwater from the wetter part of the Pacific side runs back into the sea unused. Along the shore lies a coastal desert of about 27,000 square miles, from Piura in the north to Tacna, at the border with Chile. And yet, people built a city on that coast about 5,000 years ago. It is called Karal, and it sits on a desert terrace above the green valley of the Sup River, about 90 miles north of Lima. UNESCO calls it the oldest center of civilization in the Americas. Rivers like the Sup come down from the mountains near the line, and their valleys run as strips of green across the desert. In the Moche Valley, on the north coast, canals made the dry land farmable over the centuries. There stands Chanchan, the Chimu capital, which UNESCO describes as the largest earthen city of the Americas before Columbus. A canal about 50 miles long once watered the land around it. In the south, near Nazca, a 2012 study reports that underground channels called pukios carry water to some fields. They are commonly attributed to the Nazca culture. In late 1534, Francisco Pizarro and the Spanish settlers at Jauja, high in the Andes, agreed to move toward the sea. Their town council's record cites the cold, the snow, and the long road to the coast. It also says many of the indigenous people forced to carry their supplies had died doing it. In January 1535, Pizarro chose a site partly because it had a very good port nearby. His order adds that the place lay in the middle of the land, where the indigenous people could serve the Spanish with little work. Lima was founded that month, in the Rimac Valley, near the sea and the port of Calao. Until the mid-1700s, UNESCO says, it was the capital and the most important city of Spain's dominions in South America. That put Peru's capital on the dry side of the line. It did not put most of the country's people on the coast. In 1940, Peru's census found most of them in the highlands, in the mountains the line runs along. Fewer than 3 in 10 lived in the region the census calls the coast, which covers only part of the dry side. Then, census by census, the balance tipped. The coast overtook the highlands as the most populous census region between 1961 and 1972. By the 1993 census, more than half of Peruvians lived there. By 2025, nearly six in ten lived in that coastal region. Some went east instead, into the rainforest, whose share of Peruvians more than doubled. Peru's Statistics Institute put it this way. The population had gone from predominantly Andean to mostly coastal. In a 1995 study, the same institute pointed to a shortage of farmland in the highlands and called the coast the main destination of people leaving the Andes. The political violence of the 1980s and 90s pushed many more. Peru's Truth and Reconciliation Commission estimated that about half a million people were displaced, many of them to Lima. Lima and Callao had about 650,000 people in 1940, about 1 in 10 Peruvians. By 2025, they had more than 10.5 million, about a third. In 2022, the Lima region, with Callao, produced more than 40% of Peru's economic output, by the Statistics Institute's estimate. The city drew newcomers, and many of them built their own homes at the city's edge. In 1971, a group of 180 families took over a plot of land, and more followed. The government then set out to resettle about 7,000 families on a vast, semi-desert stretch of sand south of the city. That ground became Vila El Salvador. Census figures cited by Peru's Truth Commission put about one in four of Lima's people in informal settlements by 1981. Peruvians call them barriadas. A city that size needs water all year, and its river runs low for seven months of it. From December to April, the Rimac runs high. From May to November, Peru's ombudsman reports, its natural flow falls to about a quarter of that. So Lima's water utility holds back water from the wet months in lakes and reservoirs high in the Andes and lets it out in the dry ones. By its 2007 figures, more than half of the water Lima stores for the dry season sits on the other side of the line. Those far-side lakes lie nearly 15,000 feet up in the upper Mantaro Basin, which drains toward the Amazon. The water travels about 14 miles by canal and tunnel to cross the divide. It comes out on the Pacific side, runs down the Santa Eulalia River into the Rimac, and reaches Lima's main treatment plant. The tunnel was designed to carry nearly twice the Rimac's own natural flow in the dry season. It was built between 1958 and 1962, and the system has been in service since 1963. Peru's weather service says it was built first to power a hydroelectric plant and also to steady the flow of the River Lima drinks from. The power plant, at Huinco, opened in 1964. The utility calls the tunnel the only way to bring that stored water across. The city also draws on two other rivers, the Chillon and the Lurin, and on wells. The dry season releases run from May 1 to November 30, Romulo Carjuas, a watershed analyst analyst at the utility, told its press office in remarks reported in 2024. The utility regulates 19 lakes and three large reservoirs, he said. The water makes it across the line. On the hills at the city's edge, many homes are not on the network at all. In figures reported in March 2026, Peru's water regulator counted more than 700,000 people in Lima and Callao without a connection to the water network. In the districts it studied, almost all of the homes without a connection bought water from tanker trucks, at about six times what connected homes pay. In San Juan de Miraflores, in southern Lima, Catalina Naupa, 59, told the news agency AFP in March 2025 that her water comes by truck. There are worms in the bottom of the tank, she said. To save water, she added, she washed clothes once a week or every two weeks. An official at the utility told the news agency it brings about 260 gallons of water a week to each family in the parts of the district without pipes. AFP reported that those deliveries were free. On the city's northern edge, in Puente Piedra, a German magazine described two cooperatives in a January 2024 report. In one, a 30-year-old mother of three, Virginia Martinez Morales, could pipe water from a tank to her front door. The other had 42 families without water, sewers, or electricity. After a tanker truck slid down the slope there and took three houses with it, drivers stopped going up the steepest streets. A resident, Tula Tinoco Espinosa, began carrying buckets. Most of the families there, the magazine reported, had come down from the Andean regions in search of better work. The water Lima keeps on the far side of those mountains has only one way through. In 2025, Peru's ombudsman, citing the utility's own plan, warned that, depending on a single tunnel, is a risk in a major earthquake. Lima has been planning a second tunnel since the 1990s. As of late August 2026, that tunnel still had no construction date. So, why do most Peruvians live on the dry side of that line? Not because that side has the water. First, the valleys. About 5,000 years ago, people built a city above one of them. Then, the port. In 1535, the Spanish put their capital near a very good one. Then, the people. In the 20th century, the country came down from the mountains, to the coast, and to that capital, pushed in part by a shortage of farmland and later by violence. The water had to be brought to them, by canal in the old valleys, through a tunnel across the Andes since the early 1960s, and at the city's edge, by truck. Every May 1st, the utility starts letting out its high lakes, and the water on the Amazon side crosses the divide toward the Rimac. Peru lives on the dry side of the Andes. From May to November, part of its capital's water comes from the other one.
