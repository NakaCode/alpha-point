About 83 miles off Puerto Rico, a U.S. island, lies the deepest point in the Atlantic Ocean. It's more than five miles down. Stand Mount Everest on the bottom, and its top 1,500 feet would stick out of the water. When you think "deepest ocean," you think Mariana. But that's the Pacific, about 9,500 miles away.

In 1964, a crew in a French deep-diving craft descended into the Puerto Rico Trench and reported a crowd of fish. In 2019, a submersible went back to the exact same spot. It saw none of those fish. The first person to reach the deepest point itself got there only in December 2018. And a 2019 study found that the official list of undersea features still had the Atlantic more than 1,000 feet too deep, even after sonar showed otherwise. So what is really down there, and how did anyone find out? You're at Alpha Point, and this is where the story of the bottom of the Atlantic begins.

<!-- p1 · A wire and a sound -->

On January 27, 1883, a steamer called the Blake, from the U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey, lowered a weighted wire into the sea north of Puerto Rico. Its commander was a Navy officer named Brownson. The wire went down more than five miles before it touched bottom. That was about 60 miles east of the deepest point the sonar would later find.

The wire came back up with a sample of the seafloor and a reading of the water temperature down there. The Survey's report for that year describes the bottom as brown ooze. At the time, no one was known to have brought back a sample and a temperature from that deep.

The trench itself had been found about a decade earlier, by the British research ship HMS Challenger, under Captain Nares, far to the east. Its sounding there, the depth it measured, came to about 23,000 feet. The trench runs for hundreds of miles, almost parallel to Puerto Rico's north coast.

In 1939, the cruiser USS Milwaukee bounced sound off the bottom north of Puerto Rico. It reported more than 28,000 feet, a record for the Atlantic at the time. The spot was named the Milwaukee Deep, after the ship. That summer, newspapers printed the news in a bulletin from the National Geographic Society. A sound could give a number. It couldn't show anyone what was down there.

<!-- p2 · A crew and a net -->

In 1964, people went down into the trench themselves. They rode in a French bathyscaphe, a deep-diving craft, called Archimède. It went more than four miles down. The sources disagree on exactly how deep it got. It never reached the deepest point, though. About 24,000 feet down, its crew reported a crowd of fish. They counted some 200 of them along roughly a mile of seafloor. The report, published the next year, described them as snailfish, a family of fish. Keep those fish in mind.

NOAA, the U.S. ocean and weather agency, reports that in 1970 a net brought back a small cusk-eel. A cusk-eel is a fish shaped like an eel, and this one was about eight inches long. The Australian Museum lists the same catch, from the Puerto Rico Trench. The net had gone down nearly as deep as the deepest point of the Atlantic. No one knows how deep the fish was when it swam in. A crew could see only a small patch of the trench, and a net could only bring something up. Neither could map the floor. That took a different kind of sonar.

<!-- p3 · The sonar that already knew -->

In 2002 and 2003, the U.S. Geological Survey mapped the trench with multibeam sonar, a ship's sonar that maps the seafloor in a wide strip. It took three cruises on a NOAA ship, the Ron Brown. By the end, those surveys showed the deepest part of the trench at about 27,300 feet. That was 15 years before anyone reached the deepest point.

Why is the trench so deep? Earth's outer shell is made of giant slabs called plates. In the Pacific, deep trenches lie where one plate sinks under another. Along this trench, the North American plate and the Caribbean plate mostly slide past each other, with only a little of one sinking under the other. The Caribbean plate moves east about three-quarters of an inch a year. According to the USGS, gravity over the trench falls further below normal than anywhere else on Earth. The agency says this points to an active force pulling down on the area.

By the agency's reconstruction, a tilted layer of limestone starts more than 4,000 feet above sea level. It ends about 13,000 feet below the surface. To the USGS, that tilt reflects big vertical shifts in this part of the trench and in Puerto Rico itself. Under the trench's deepest part, rotated blocks of rock indicate a sinking floor. That, the agency suggests, is why the trench is so deep. On the trench's walls, the agency's maps showed something else.

<!-- p4 · What came ashore -->

The USGS says those walls show many old underwater landslides, and that the risk of a tsunami set off by a landslide like those appears to be high. In October 1918, an earthquake struck the Mona Passage, west of Puerto Rico. It wasn't in the trench. NOAA's tsunami database puts it at magnitude 7.1. Its tsunami ran as high as 20 feet up Puerto Rico's west coast. The quake and its tsunami killed more than 100 people.

Farther east, an island holds a sign of what the trench itself can do. Anegada is a low island in the British Virgin Islands. Hundreds of coral boulders lie far inland there, along roughly six miles of its north shore, the side that faces the trench. By measuring the uranium in that coral, a 2025 study dated the tsunami that left them to sometime between 1381 and 1391. That's about a century before Columbus reached the Caribbean.

The earthquake behind that wave struck the eastern part of this trench, far from its deepest point. Computer models put it at magnitude 8 or more. The scientists who dated the coral call the wave the only known sign that this stretch of the trench has produced a quake that big. They think quakes like that come thousands of years apart.

<!-- p5 · A robot, a sonar and a man alone -->

In 2015, a NOAA robot filmed the trench's south wall, almost 20,000 feet down. That's a long way east of its deepest point. The robot, Deep Discoverer, worked from the ship Okeanos Explorer, on one of a dozen dives it made on that voyage. Soon after, a problem with the robot cut this one short.

In December 2018, the Five Deeps Expedition came to map the trench with a modern multibeam sonar, on a ship called the Pressure Drop. The expedition's goal was to reach the deepest point of each of the five oceans. To get the sonar right, the team measured how fast sound travels at every depth, from the surface to the floor.

By the expedition's survey, the deepest point of the Atlantic is about 27,500 feet down. Give or take about 16 feet. That point lies about 83 miles from the nearest coast of Puerto Rico, off Aguadilla, in the island's northwest. More than three million people live in Puerto Rico.

On Wednesday, December 19, 2018, Victor Vescovo took a submersible called Limiting Factor down to the spot the sonar had just mapped. He made the trip alone, in his own sub, built to reach the bottom of any ocean. The trip down took about two and a half hours. He was the first person to reach the deepest point of the Atlantic. When he climbed out, a reporter on board heard him say: "One down!" The sub's own gauges confirmed the sonar's number, within about 7 feet. That number was not the one in the official list.

<!-- p6 · The number on the list -->

In 2019, two scientists, Heather Stewart and Alan Jamieson, found that the official list of undersea features had the Milwaukee Deep at about 28,700 feet. That's almost exactly what the newspapers reported in 1939. The floor the expedition's sonar found is about 1,200 feet shallower. And the USGS sonar had shown a shallower floor since 2003.

Stewart and Jamieson checked the published depths against sonar data that was already public. Off Puerto Rico, the real floor came out shallower every time. They wrote that numbers like these had spread, uncorrected, through online sources and the scientific literature. At the spot the old listing pointed to, their study found about 24,400 feet, the top of a ridge.

Their study came out before the expedition's own results, and it put the deepest point somewhere else. Using public data from eight older surveys, it placed that point about 36 miles west of the sonar's spot, in the Dominican Republic's waters. The expedition's results, published in 2021, placed it in U.S. waters off Puerto Rico, about 100 miles from the Dominican Republic. Those U.S. waters are the exclusive economic zone, a band of sea where the U.S. controls the resources.

As of October 2026, the official list of undersea features doesn't include the Milwaukee Deep. The expedition's scientists write that all of its sonar data went to Seabed 2030, the project to map the whole ocean floor by 2030. The expedition also took its sonar, and its sub, to the other end of the Atlantic.

<!-- p7 · The other contender -->

The Atlantic has another contender for its deepest point: the South Sandwich Trench, far to the south, where one plate dives under another about three inches a year. That is three to four times the pace of the plates off Puerto Rico. The islands there are a chain of small volcanoes, with no permanent residents. Both Britain and Argentina claim them. In August 2026, a Landsat satellite caught the heat of a lava lake in the crater of Mount Michael, one of those volcanoes.

The expedition mapped this trench with its sonar in 2019. Before that, about nine-tenths of it had never been mapped. Stewart and Jamieson's study noted the depth most often published for its deepest point. It was deeper than the sonar's number for Puerto Rico. The sonar settled that. The trench's deepest point, the Meteor Deep, is about 370 feet shallower than the one off Puerto Rico. The expedition mapped it but didn't dive there. The name Meteor Deep is old, taken from a German research ship. Since 2021, that name has officially marked the spot the sonar found, about 30 miles from where older sources put it.

In 2023, the International Hydrographic Organization recognized the Southern Ocean. It begins at 60 degrees south. By that line, the Meteor Deep, well to the north, is in the Atlantic. The organization says the line carries no political meaning, and not every country uses it. Argentina, for one, disagrees.

The trench's southern tip crosses the line. Just south of the line lies the deepest point of the Southern Ocean, the Factorian Deep. The expedition's sub, the Limiting Factor, reached the bottom there in 2019, and that's where the odd name comes from. Its neighbor, about 20 miles away, got the other half of the name: Limiting Deep. Off Puerto Rico, the 1964 crew's fish were waiting for a second look.

<!-- p8 · The second look -->

In 2019, the same sub went back to the spot where that crew had reported their fish. It was the craft that had carried Vescovo to the deepest point the year before. Where the 1964 crew had counted about 200 fish, it saw no snailfish. It found sea cucumbers instead, spread about as thickly as the fish the crew had reported. From the outside, the two look a lot alike in color, texture and shape.

In a 2020 paper on that return visit, five authors concluded that the "fish" of 1964 were sea cucumbers. It's a conclusion drawn from the count and the look, and the animals the 1964 crew saw that day can't be checked again. One of the five was Vescovo.

By NOAA's estimate, no fish lives much deeper than about 27,500 feet. The net that brought up the cusk-eel in 1970 had gone down nearly that far. The deepest point in the Atlantic, about 83 miles off Puerto Rico, sits right at that edge.
