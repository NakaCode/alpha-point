About 83 miles off Puerto Rico, a U.S. island, lies the deepest point in the Atlantic Ocean. It's more than five miles down. Stand Mount Everest on the bottom, and its top 1,500 feet would stick out of the water. When you think "deepest ocean," you think Mariana. But that's the Pacific, about 9,500 miles away.

In 1964, a French crew dove into the Puerto Rico Trench and reported a crowd of fish. In 2019, a submersible went back to the exact same spot. It found no fish at all. The first person to reach the deepest point itself got there only in December 2018. And for decades, the official list of undersea features gave that point a number about 1,200 feet too deep, even after sonar showed otherwise. So what is really down there, and how did anyone find out? You're at Alpha Point, and this is where the story of the bottom of the Atlantic begins.

<!-- p1 · A wire and a sound -->

On January 27, 1883, a steamer called the Blake, from the U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey, lowered a weighted wire into the sea north of Puerto Rico. Its commander was a Navy officer named Brownson, who had taken charge of the ship a few months earlier. The wire went down more than five miles before it touched bottom. That was about 60 miles east of the deepest point the sonar would later find.

It came back up with a sample of the seafloor and a reading of the water temperature down there. The Survey's report for that year describes the bottom as brown ooze. At the time, that was believed to be the greatest depth a sample had ever been brought up from.

The trench itself had been found eight years earlier, by the British research ship HMS Challenger, under Captain Nares, far to the east. Its sounding there, the depth it measured, came to about 23,000 feet. The trench runs for hundreds of miles, almost parallel to Puerto Rico's north coast.

In 1939, the cruiser USS Milwaukee bounced sound off the bottom north of Puerto Rico. It reported more than 28,000 feet, a record for the Atlantic at the time. The spot was named the Milwaukee Deep, after the ship. That summer, newspapers printed the news in a bulletin from the National Geographic Society. A sound could give a number. It couldn't show anyone what was down there.

<!-- p2 · A crew and a net -->

In 1964, people went down into the trench themselves. They rode in a French bathyscaphe, a deep-diving craft, called Archimède. It went more than four miles down. Exactly how deep, the sources disagree. It never reached the deepest point, though. About 24,000 feet down, the French crew reported a crowd of fish. Some 200 of them, along about a mile of seafloor. Their account, published the next year, described them as snailfish, a family of fish. Keep those fish in mind.

NOAA, the U.S. ocean and weather agency, records that in 1970 a net hauled up from the trench brought back a small cusk-eel. A cusk-eel is a fish shaped like an eel, and this one was about eight inches long. The Australian Museum lists the same catch. The net had gone down nearly as deep as the deepest point of the Atlantic. How deep the fish was when it swam in, no one knows. A crew could see only a small patch of the trench, and a net could only bring something up. Neither could map the floor. That took a different kind of sonar.

<!-- p3 · The sonar that already knew -->

In 2002 and 2003, the U.S. Geological Survey mapped the trench with multibeam sonar, a ship's sonar that maps the seafloor in a wide strip. It took three cruises on a NOAA ship, the Ron Brown. By the end, those surveys showed the deepest part of the trench at about 27,300 feet. That was 15 years before anyone reached the deepest point.

Why is the trench so deep? Earth's outer shell is made of giant slabs called plates. Along the trench, the North American plate and the Caribbean plate mostly slide past each other, with only a little of one sinking under the other. The Caribbean plate moves east about three-quarters of an inch a year. By the USGS's measure, the trench has the most negative gravity anomaly on Earth. Gravity there falls further below normal than anywhere else.

The agency found a tilted slab of limestone that starts more than 4,000 feet above sea level. It ends about 13,000 feet below the surface. The USGS also reads rotated blocks of rock deep in the trench as signs that the trench has been sinking. On the trench's walls, its maps showed something else.

<!-- p4 · What came ashore -->

The USGS says those walls show many old underwater landslides, and that the risk of a tsunami set off by a landslide like those appears to be high. In October 1918, an earthquake struck the Mona Passage, west of Puerto Rico. It wasn't in the trench. NOAA's tsunami database puts it at magnitude 7.1. Its tsunami ran as high as 20 feet up Puerto Rico's west coast. The quake and its tsunami killed more than 100 people.

Farther east, an island holds a sign of what the trench itself can do. Anegada is a low, flat island in the British Virgin Islands. Hundreds of coral boulders lie far inland there, along about six miles of its north shore, the side that faces the trench. By measuring the uranium in that coral, a 2025 study dated the tsunami that left them to sometime between 1381 and 1391. That's about a century before Columbus reached the Caribbean. The authors suspect the coral died in summer or fall.

The earthquake behind it struck the eastern part of this trench, far from its deepest point. Computer models put it at magnitude 8 or more. The scientists who dated the coral call it the only known sign that this stretch of the trench can do that. That quake wasn't behind the deadly tsunami of 1918, either. They think quakes like that come thousands of years apart. To see the trench's walls, you need a camera down there.

<!-- p5 · A robot, a sonar and a man alone -->

In 2015, a NOAA robot filmed the trench's south wall, almost 20,000 feet down. That's in the eastern part of the trench, a long way from its deepest point. The robot, Deep Discoverer, worked from NOAA's ship Okeanos Explorer, on one of a dozen dives it made on that voyage. Soon after, a problem with the robot cut this one short.

In December 2018, the Five Deeps Expedition came to map the trench with a modern multibeam sonar, on a ship called the Pressure Drop. To get the sonar right, the ship lowered instruments to measure how fast sound travels at every depth, from the surface to the floor. Most of the seafloor it covered had been mapped before.

By the expedition's survey, the deepest point of the Atlantic is about 27,500 feet down. Give or take about 16 feet. The scientists give that margin with 95 percent confidence. They built their map as a grid of squares about 250 feet across. The results were published in 2021. That point lies about 83 miles from the nearest coast of Puerto Rico, off Aguadilla, in the island's northwest. More than three million people live in Puerto Rico.

On Wednesday, December 19, 2018, Victor Vescovo took a submersible called Limiting Factor down to the spot the sonar had just mapped. He made the trip alone, in a sub built to reach the bottom of any ocean. The trip down took about two and a half hours. He was the first person to reach the deepest point of the Atlantic. The sub's own gauges confirmed the sonar's number, within about 7 feet. That number was not the one in the official list.

<!-- p6 · The number on the list -->

Until at least 2019, the official list of undersea features had the Milwaukee Deep at about 28,700 feet. That's almost exactly what the newspapers reported in 1939. The floor the sonar found is about 1,200 feet shallower. And the USGS sonar had shown a shallower floor since 2003. As late as 2011, a press release for a planned deep-diving sub listed more than 28,000 feet.

In 2019, two scientists, Heather Stewart and Alan Jamieson, checked the published depths against sonar data that was already public. In their check, the real floor came out shallower every time. They wrote that numbers like these had spread, uncorrected, through online sources and the scientific literature. At the spot the old listing pointed to, their study found about 24,400 feet, the top of a ridge.

Their study also put the deepest point somewhere else. Working from public data from eight older surveys, it placed that point about 36 miles west of the sonar's spot, in waters claimed by the Dominican Republic. Their study came out before the expedition's own results. Those were published in 2021, and they placed the deepest point in U.S. waters off Puerto Rico, about 100 miles from the Dominican Republic. U.S. waters, in that sense, means the exclusive economic zone: a band of sea up to about 230 miles out, where the U.S. controls the resources.

As of October 2026, the international list of undersea feature names no longer includes the Milwaukee Deep. The two scientists had asked for that name to fall out of use. The expedition's scientists write that all of its sonar data went to Seabed 2030, the project to map the whole ocean floor by 2030. The expedition also took its sonar, and its sub, to the other end of the Atlantic.

<!-- p7 · The other contender -->

The Atlantic has one other contender for its deepest point: the South Sandwich Trench, far to the south, where one plate dives under another about three inches a year. That is three to four times the pace of the plates off Puerto Rico. The islands there are a chain of small volcanoes, with no one living on them. Both Britain and Argentina claim them. In August 2026, a Landsat satellite caught the heat of a lava lake in the crater of Mount Michael, one of those volcanoes.

The expedition mapped this trench with its sonar in 2019. Before that, about nine-tenths of it had never been mapped. The depth most often published for its deepest point, the same 2019 study found, was deeper than the sonar's number for Puerto Rico. The sonar settled that. The trench's deepest point, the Meteor Deep, is about 370 feet shallower than the one off Puerto Rico. The expedition mapped it but didn't dive there.

The name Meteor Deep is old, after a German research ship. Since 2021, it has officially belonged to the spot the sonar found, about 30 miles from where older books put it. The expedition had wanted to call it Pressure Deep, after its own ship, the Pressure Drop.

In 2023, the International Hydrographic Organization recognized the Southern Ocean. It begins at 60 degrees south. The organization says the line carries no political meaning, and not every country uses it. Argentina, for one, disagrees. By that line, the Meteor Deep, well to the north, is in the Atlantic.

The trench's southern tip crosses the line. Just south of the line lies the deepest point of the Southern Ocean, the Factorian Deep. The Limiting Factor reached its bottom in 2019, and that's where the odd name comes from. Its neighbor, about 20 miles away, got the other half of the name: Limiting Deep. Off Puerto Rico, the French crew's fish were waiting for a second look.

<!-- p8 · The second look -->

In 2019, the same sub went back to the spot where the French crew had reported their fish. It was the craft that had carried Vescovo to the deepest point the year before. Where the French had counted about 200 fish, it found none. It found sea cucumbers instead, about as many as the fish the French had counted. From the outside, the two look a lot alike: similar color, similar texture, similar shape.

The scientists who went back concluded that the "fish" of 1964 were sea cucumbers. It's a conclusion drawn from the count and the look, and the animals the French saw that day can't be checked again. The paper that reported it came out in 2020, with five authors. One of them was Vescovo.

By NOAA's estimate, no fish lives much deeper than about 27,500 feet. The net that brought up the cusk-eel in 1970 had gone down nearly that far. The deepest point in the Atlantic, about 83 miles off Puerto Rico, sits right at that edge.
