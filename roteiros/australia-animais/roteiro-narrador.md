Out on the cracked floodplains of the Australian outback lives the inland taipan, the land snake with the most potent venom on Earth. That ranking comes from a test on lab mice. But the inland taipan isn't even the snake that kills the most Australians.

And it has plenty of relatives. By our count, about two in three of Australia's snake species belong to the same venomous family it does.

Now look at the official death records of a country famous for its deadly animals. Over ten years, from 2016 to 2025, every venomous animal in Australia combined killed 85 people. Spiders killed none. Australia's roads killed more people in an average month of 2025 than venom did in that whole decade.

So how does a country with so many venomous snakes end up with so few deaths?

You're at Alpha Point, and this is where the story of Australia's venom begins.

<!-- ato 1 · o registro e a fama -->

You've probably heard the question. Why does Australia have so many dangerous animals? People ask it about the snakes, the spiders, the jellyfish, the crocodiles and the sharks, usually all in one breath. Before the why, though, it helps to settle how dangerous those animals actually are. That part has been counted.

Every death registered in Australia is filed under a cause, and Australia's statistics bureau publishes the totals. Go back to that decade of records, and take the venomous animals one at a time. Venomous snakes killed 35 people. Bees and wasps killed 42, more than the snakes. We'll come back to the bees.

If you're adding along, that leaves eight deaths. Most of them are filed under what the death codes call "other venomous arthropods," and the examples the code gives are ants and caterpillars. The rest are filed under venomous marine animals, a heading that covers jellyfish, coral and sea anemones. The records don't say which animal it was.

Spiders don't appear at all: not one death, in any of those years. It isn't that spiders don't bite. Over a little more than a decade, one study found, spider bites put Australians in the hospital about 12,000 times. None of those bites killed anyone.

Set every one of those deaths against the country's population, and the odds of being killed by a venomous animal come to about one in three million a year. The math is ours, from the bureau's figures. It's a real risk, and a very small one.

The famous list is real, then, and the death toll is short. One scientist has put that gap into words. Kevin Arbuckle, an evolutionary biologist at Swansea University, says Australia's image as the home of highly venomous animals is, "for the most part," overstated. Its variety of venomous animals, he says, is "not unusual at all for a largely tropical area." Box jellyfish, he points out, live in warm seas well beyond Australia.

That doesn't make the danger invented. The animals on that list can kill, and the records show they do. And there's one exception, he says: the snakes.

So start there: to see how Australia ended up with so many venomous snakes, look at where the continent has been.

<!-- ato 2 · as cobras que vieram da Ásia -->

Go to the bottom of the globe. Some 80 to 85 million years ago, Australia began to pull away from Antarctica. It was the final big break in an old supercontinent called Gondwana.

Around 45 million years ago, Australia started moving north, and over the millions of years that followed, deep ocean opened up behind it. Follow it up the map: a whole continent on the move, with water on every side. For tens of millions of years, it traveled alone.

You might assume nothing new could reach it until it ran into something. But before Australia ever reached Asia, something had already made the crossing.

A family of venomous snakes arrived from Asia. They're called elapids: front-fanged snakes, the family that cobras belong to. Its earliest branches are mostly Asian.

Genetic dating puts the family's spread across the world's continents at roughly 25 to 30 million years ago. That's an estimate, and it falls before Australia and Asia met. By that estimate, the snakes got to Australia before Australia got to Asia. How they crossed the water, the dating doesn't say.

Elapids seem to have been the first venomous snakes to reach the continent. And notice who wasn't there. Australia had no vipers, the family of rattlesnakes, and none of the other modern snake families had arrived. In Australia, elapids fill most of the roles a snake can play on land, and some live in the sea. The inland taipan is one of them.

That's the family our count was picking up. Worldwide, by the same count, elapids and vipers together make up only about one in five snake species, in roughly equal shares. In Australia, elapids alone are the clear majority, and there isn't a single viper.

Then, about 23 million years ago, Australia's leading edge began running into Southeast Asia, and a chain of islands formed between the two. On a map, those islands look like stepping stones. Very few mammals ever made it across them. The only ones that crossed on their own and took hold were rodents and bats. So Australia ended up with kangaroos and koalas, and with no native cats and no hoofed animals at all.

So that's where the snakes came from: one venomous family that arrived early, on a continent with no vipers.

<!-- ato 3 · a taipan e o vazio -->

Then the continent dried out. Its interior began turning arid about 15 million years ago, and the full deserts of the center appeared only in the past few million years.

Find two rivers in that dry interior, the Diamantina and Cooper Creek. Their floodplains are cracked clay, and they're the plains this story opened on. This is where the inland taipan lives.

It hunts rats, and it shelters in their burrows and in cracks in the ground. The rats, as it happens, descend from rodents that crossed from Asia too. When the taipan strikes, it bites once, pulls back, and waits for the rat to die. The scientists who study these venoms think that's probably an adaptation to its prey: a rat can bite back.

Its title, though, was won indoors. In 1979, researchers tested the venoms of dangerous snakes on lab mice. They measured the dose that kills half the mice in a test, what scientists call the LD50. The smaller the dose, the more potent the venom, and the inland taipan's came out on top.

Keep two things in mind about that ranking. Researchers have since criticized mouse tests like that one, partly because they don't reflect what happens when a snake bites a person. And a ranking of venoms, drop for drop, says nothing about how often a snake and a person meet. This snake is, in the words of a South Australian government fact sheet, "usually quite shy."

With a title like that, you might expect a long medical record, and it's a short one. By 2017, doctors had written up only about a dozen cases of someone bitten by an inland taipan and injected with venom. Only three of those bites came from a snake in the wild.

Then check the address. The country around those floodplains is called the Channel Country, and it covers about 117,000 square miles. At the 2021 census, about a thousand people lived there. That's roughly one person for every hundred square miles. It's a great deal of land, with very few neighbors.

The snake with the most potent venom on land has almost no record of hurting people. The snake that actually kills Australians lives where far more people do.

The test that crowned the inland taipan put another Australian snake near the top, too: the brown snake. Brown snakes and taipans share a toxin no other snakes have.

What they don't share is an address. Take one of them, the eastern brown snake. According to the Queensland Museum, it's found over most of Queensland, New South Wales and Victoria. It turns up in every habitat except rainforest, and it has adapted well to farmed, grazed and semi-urban land. Put simply, it does well where people farm and build.

Look at who lives nearby. At that same census, those three states had almost 20 million people. Per square mile, that's more than a thousand times as many people as the Channel Country.

Both snakes sit near the top of the same test. What differs is who lives around them.

A separate national study of snakebites, from 2005 to 2015, counted 23 deaths. Seventeen were put down to brown snakes. None was put down to the inland taipan.

For a country with so many venomous snakes, that toll is small. Small, compared with what? Compared with India, for one, where the World Health Organization cites an estimate of about 58,000 snakebite deaths a year. Australia's records show three or four a year. Work it out per person, and India's rate is hundreds of times Australia's.

You might be thinking: that's the antivenom. Partly. The answer starts before any hospital, though. In Australia, a snake actually putting venom into a person is rare to begin with. That same national study found fewer than a hundred bites a year that injected venom, far fewer than earlier estimates had suggested.

Then there's the medicine: Australia has made antivenom since 1930, and the government's health service says one is available for every venomous snake native to the country. And there's Australia's official first-aid advice for snakebite: bandage the whole limb firmly, and keep the person from moving. Don't wash the bite, either: venom left on the skin can help identify the snake. The bandage isn't a treatment. It slows the venom on its way into the blood, and that buys time.

The doctors who study all this are careful about it. No one has compared the bandage with other first aid in the field, and as of that national study, no controlled trial of the antivenoms had been published.

Medicine is part of why so few people die of snakebite. It isn't the whole of it, and nothing we've read measures how much.

All of that is on land. Up in the tropical north, the danger is somewhere else.

<!-- ato 5 · o norte e as aranhas -->

It's in the water, and it comes with a season. By a 2005 count, the box jellyfish had been blamed for around 70 deaths in tropical Australian waters over the years. It starts life as tiny polyps in tidal creeks, and it comes out to sea at the start of each "stinger season," which runs from October to June. This was Arbuckle's example, remember: box jellyfish live in warm seas well beyond Australia. What Australia added was a treatment, the world's first antivenom for a jellyfish.

One of the most famous animals on the list doesn't live in the outback or in the sea. It lives around Sydney.

Funnel-web spiders are old. Their family goes back 150 to 200 million years, long before Australia broke away from Antarctica, and long before there were any primates. So why is their venom so deadly to us? A team that studied the toxin thinks it started out as a weapon for catching insects, and that the males turned it into a defense against small animals like bandicoots, birds and lizards. Its lethal effect on humans, in their words, is "an unfortunate evolutionary coincidence." Dogs and cats are insensitive to it.

The bites mostly come from males that leave their burrows to look for females, and most happen between November and March. Severe cases have only been recorded in New South Wales and southern Queensland.

For a long time, those bites could kill. Before 1981, funnel-web spiders killed at least 13 people, by one review of the recorded cases. That year, an antivenom came into use. The review found no confirmed death after it. Where an expert had identified the spider, the antivenom worked completely in almost every case.

The redback, another of Australia's venomous spiders, is a less tidy story. Its antivenom was made by a scientist who dissected the venom glands of hundreds of spiders to do it. Redback bites killed at least 14 people before that antivenom arrived, in 1956, and there's been no confirmed death since. A later trial, though, found that the antivenom didn't significantly ease pain or other symptoms. So it isn't clear that the antivenom is what keeps redback bites from killing.

So the most potent venom on land rarely touches anyone, and spiders are missing from a decade of death records. Then what does kill Australians?

<!-- ato 6 · quem mata de verdade -->

In the venom records, the top of the list isn't a snake. It's bees and wasps. Those deaths usually come from anaphylaxis, a severe allergic reaction, and in one study of those deaths, fewer than half of the people reached medical care before they died.

There's a twist in that, too. The insect behind most of Australia's fatal sting allergies is the honeybee, and the honeybee isn't native. It came from Europe, by ship, in 1822.

Then there are the animals with no venom at all. They killed more Australians than the venomous ones did: 154 over the same decade. Australia's national database of coroners' cases makes the same point: deaths from snakes, spiders, bees and the rest "remain relatively rare" next to deaths associated with other species.

If you've been waiting for the sharks and the crocodiles, this is where they turn up, and they have company. The death records count dogs and other mammals, animals in the sea, sharks among them, and crocodiles. On farms, a separate study found, most fatal incidents with animals involved horses or cattle.

The sharks don't get a number of their own: the records count them with all the other sea animals that have no venom. The crocodiles killed 13 people in that decade. The saltwater crocodile isn't only Australia's: it lives from Sri Lanka to northern Australia. After decades of commercial hunting, the Northern Territory protected it in 1971, and it came back.

Heat and water kill far more Australians than venom does. Over that same decade, extreme natural heat killed 202 people, and drowning in rivers, lakes and the sea killed more than a thousand. None of that is on the famous list. Neither are the roads we started with.

Which brings the reputation back, and Kevin Arbuckle with it. He has wondered whether part of Australia's image comes from the country's "scientific capital" and its "excellent infrastructure" for public health and medical care. One way to read that: a country that studies and treats its venom this closely ends up known for it. It's a thought, not a finding. That same medicine, though, is part of why so few people die. Only part. The snake with the most potent venom lives where very few people do, and a snake putting venom into a person is rare to begin with. After that comes the medicine. And none of it runs by itself.

<!-- fecho · o parque das funnel-web: o gesto de quem mora ao lado da aranha (a chamada de comentário, só de tela, entra depois do último fato) -->

North of Sydney, on the Central Coast, the Australian Reptile Park milks funnel-web spiders for their venom. It's the only supplier of funnel-web venom for antivenom in Australia. Only adult males are milked, because their venom, the park says, is significantly more potent. Those are the same spiders that wander out and do most of the biting.

It takes about 150 milkings to make a single vial, so the park needs spiders, and it gets them from its neighbors. It asks people around Sydney, the Central Coast and Newcastle to catch the funnel-webs they find, safely, and bring them in. During the COVID lockdowns, almost no spiders were donated. In January 2026, the park said it was down to about half its usual supply.

Since the antivenom program began, more than 40 years ago, the park says, no one has died from a funnel-web bite.

Somewhere between Sydney and Newcastle, someone finds a funnel-web. They catch it, safely, and carry it to one of the park's drop-off points. The spider that once killed people here is caught by the people it lives next to. And its venom goes into the cure.
