On August twenty-seventh, nineteen seventy-four, Alaska voters approved moving their state capital out of Juneau. Half a century later, the capital is still in Juneau. So what happened between that vote and the move that never came? The answer comes down to one line that showed up on later ballots: the price.
You can't drive to Juneau from the rest of the continent. Its roads run about forty-three miles north, to Cascade Point, and stop. Every car, passenger and load of freight comes and goes by air or by sea.
For the people who wanted the capital out, that was the point. One of them, Harris, chaired a committee pushing the move. He made the case in the official voter pamphlet of nineteen eighty-two. Travel there, he wrote, "is so expensive and time-consuming that few ordinary citizens are able to go there."
Juneau got the capital early, and the rest of Alaska grew up somewhere else. The city began as a gold camp.
The capital came in stages. In nineteen hundred, a federal law named Juneau the temporary seat of government, but the government stayed in Sitka, the old Russian capital. The governor's office moved to Juneau in nineteen oh-six. And the nineteen twelve law creating the Territory of Alaska was plain: "the capital of the Territory of Alaska shall be at the city of Juneau, Alaska."
Alaska's population, though, moved north. A railroad work camp became Anchorage. Around World War Two, military bases went up at Anchorage and Fairbanks. The Alaska and Glenn highways tied Anchorage to the continent's roads. The state labor department says the Anchorage and Fairbanks areas held roughly fifteen percent of Alaskans in nineteen forty. By nineteen sixty, they held more than half.
Voters rejected moving the capital in nineteen sixty and nineteen sixty-two. In August nineteen seventy-four, they approved it, through a ballot initiative, a law that voters themselves put on the ballot. Anchorage's districts voted eighty percent yes. The Southeast, around Juneau, voted about ninety-four percent no. Fairbanks split nearly even.
The nineteen seventy-four law also set a rule: up to three candidate sites, none within thirty miles of Anchorage or Fairbanks. The state Supreme Court upheld the initiative that same year. As the attorney general summed it up in nineteen ninety-three, the premise was a planned capital away from the big cities.
That nineteen seventy-four vote decided the capital would leave. What remained was choosing the place, and saying what it would cost.
In November nineteen seventy-six, voters picked the place. The ballot offered three sites. The winner, with a little more than half the vote, lay near the town of Willow, in the Mat-Sue Valley. The site was about seventy road miles north of Anchorage, and five miles east of Willow itself.
In Fairbanks, a group wanted to know the price first. In early nineteen seventy-seven, they organized the Frank committee, its name short for "Frustrated, Responsible Alaskans Needing Knowledge." Their initiative said the state could not spend money on the move until voters approved bonds covering its costs through nineteen ninety-two. A bond is money the state borrows and pays back over many years.
The move's supporters answered the Frank initiative in the official nineteen seventy-eight voter pamphlet. "Its true purpose is to prohibit the move to Willow," their statement said.
Then came the price. In November nineteen seventy-eight, the Frank initiative went to the voters. On the same ballot sat the money to build: up to nine hundred sixty-six million dollars in bonds for the new capital, almost a billion dollars. The same voters made two choices. They passed the Frank initiative, with nearly fifty-six percent. And they rejected the bonds, by almost three to one. Willow had a site, and no money approved to build on it.
