In nineteen eighty-one, the legislature rewrote the rules. There would be no bond vote this time. Instead, voters would get one last question in November nineteen eighty-two: could the state spend the money the move would take? The ballot printed the state's estimate: about two point eight billion dollars, counted through nineteen ninety-four. The estimate included nearly five hundred ninety million dollars to compensate Juneau residents for the move.
It was winner take all. A yes vote meant building at Willow. A no vote meant Juneau stayed the capital.
Harris, the committee chair quoted earlier, called the figures inflated. Jim Clark, against the move, reminded voters of the bonds they turned down in nineteen seventy-eight.
The pro-move side had a mascot, Access Annie. Lee Stoops, a legislative aide who lived in Juneau, answered with a rival: Football Fannie. In twenty twenty-six, he told Anchorage Daily News columnist David Reamer why. "I decided to just make fun of the whole process and the amount of money they wanted to spend to move the capital and build in Willow." Fannie's first ad priced twenty-eight N F L teams and three domed stadiums at eight hundred million dollars. The ad called that about a quarter of the Willow price tag.
A Dittman Research poll in late September had the move ahead, fifty-two percent to forty-five. In November nineteen eighty-two, the no side won, with nearly fifty-three percent.
Why voters said no is still argued. What they said no to is not. It was a printed price. Stoops says the satire decided it. Reamer calls it one factor, at the very least. Before the vote, pollster Dave Dittman didn't think the price tag weighed that heavily. In nineteen ninety, state labor economist Brian Rae wrote that the expense "finally persuaded voters to reject the plans." Reamer adds that opponents spent more than a million dollars.
By the ballot's own terms, the nineteen eighty-two no vote also repealed every move law, from the nineteen seventy-four initiative to the Frank initiative.
The move came back in nineteen ninety-four. The new site was Wasilla, another Mat-Sue Valley town, and the ballot carried no price. In the official election pamphlet, supporters said Wasilla was on the road system. Opponents recalled the nineteen eighty-two estimate of about two point eight billion dollars. "This time," the statement against it said, "no price tag is attached."
In November nineteen ninety-four, nearly fifty-five percent voted no on Wasilla. It lost without a price tag, so the price can't explain every defeat. The same ballot carried a second initiative: the right to know the cost first. It passed with about seventy-seven percent.
That initiative revived the nineteen seventy-eight idea, and its wording is still law. State money can move the capital or the legislature only after voters approve bonds for twelve years of costs. The rule covers the legislature too: lawmakers could move their sessions even with the capital still in Juneau. The law calls for a nine-member commission to tally the costs, including social, economic and environmental ones. The stated purpose is the people's "right to know."
Eight years later, voters rejected moving the legislative sessions to the Mat-Sue Valley, by about two to one. That measure would also have dropped the cost rule for moving the legislature.
In twenty twenty-five, a House bill to hold legislative sessions in Anchorage proposed repealing the whole cost law, for the capital as well as the legislature. As of September twenty twenty-six, the bill hadn't left its first committee. Juneau's state senator, Jesse Keel, told Kay-too in twenty twenty-five: "It's a little bit like a zombie movie. It's never all the way dead."
