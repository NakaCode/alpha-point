Then there's the road. If getting to Juneau is the complaint, why not just build one? When a federal project could significantly change the environment, federal law requires an environmental impact statement, a study of what the project would change. The twenty eighteen study for a Juneau road says travel limits feed the perception of an isolated capital. Capital move supporters, it adds, often cite that.
Look at a map and you'll see the problem with a road. Behind Juneau, an icefield straddles the border with British Columbia, the Canadian province next door. That leaves two corridors: the Taku River valley to the east, and Lynn Canal to the north. British Columbia said in nineteen ninety-three and two thousand three it wasn't interested in the Taku. Lynn Canal is a long fjord between steep mountains, not a dug canal.
Any road north would have to reach Haines or Skagway, at the top of that fjord. From there, the roads out cross into Canada and meet the Alaska Highway in the Yukon. With no such road, Juneau's official highway link is the state ferry system, called the Alaska Marine Highway.
In January two thousand, Governor Tony Knowles picked the state's preferred option: a road up the east side of Lynn Canal. Because of its high cost, he said, he wouldn't actively pursue it while he was in office. That October, Juneau voters split almost evenly. Better ferries finished just seventy-nine votes ahead of a road.
Highway officials chose a version of that route in two thousand six. It stopped short of Haines and Skagway, so drivers would finish the trip on a ferry. The study counts forty-one avalanche paths crossing it, and puts its cost at about six hundred eighty million dollars. In two thousand nine, a federal court threw out that choice. The state first had to weigh better service with its existing ferries.
Then the money ran short. The study notes that for a decade, oil supplied about ninety percent of the state's unrestricted general fund revenue. That's the money that runs state government. From a high in twenty thirteen to a low in early twenty sixteen, oil prices fell by more than three quarters. In December twenty sixteen, Governor Bill Walker said he preferred what the study calls the No Action alternative: keep the ferries and build no road. He cited the state's fiscal crisis.
The Juneau Assembly, the city council, still backed the road in January twenty seventeen, six to three. Assembly member Maria Gladyshevsky voted no. "It would be a lot harder for me," she said, "if it were actually a road to Skagway or a road to Haines but it's a road to another ferry."
The No Action alternative became final in June twenty eighteen.
In the end, the road ran into a price too. The twenty eighteen decision pointed to the state's budget, and to a public fight over the road, a fight that was never settled. The road north was never built, and Juneau's highway still stops about forty-three miles north, at Cascade Point.
So what happened to that yes vote in nineteen seventy-four? The question changed. In nineteen seventy-four, the question was whether to move. Voters said yes. Then it became how much. Both times a price went on the ballot, voters said no, though why is still debated. Since nineteen ninety-four, the law asks how much first. For the capital to leave, voters would have to approve its price before state money could move it.
That yes vote was real. It just never came with a price voters would approve.
