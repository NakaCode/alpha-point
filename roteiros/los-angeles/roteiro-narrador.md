Los Angeles is America's second-largest city. And by the port's own count, its harbor has handled more container traffic than any other in the Western Hemisphere every year from 2000 through 2024. But in 1886, an Army engineer looked at the shallow estuary there and wrote that it "can never be made a harbor for deep-water ships."

Now look at the city's outline in 1906. To reach that water, Los Angeles ran south through a strip about half a mile wide, for about fifteen miles, across county land. It stopped at the edge of two other cities, short of the water. Until 1909, Los Angeles had no coastline of its own. So why is Los Angeles shaped like this? You're at Alpha Point, and this is where the story of the strip that took Los Angeles to its port begins.

<!-- p1 · A city fourteen miles from the beach -->

Start with where the city was born: on a plain, not on the water. Los Angeles became a city in April 1850, about twenty-eight square miles around the old Plaza. From the Plaza, the nearest open beach is about fourteen miles away. Wilmington, where the harbor is today, is about nineteen.

And the coast it faced had no harbor. In 1886, Colonel Mendell of the Army Corps of Engineers wrote that the only deep-water harbors in California were San Francisco and San Diego. San Pedro Bay, in between, was an open anchorage, exposed to the southeast and south, where the winter storms came from.

In 1835, Richard Henry Dana's ship anchored about three miles out, and the crew called it the worst place they had seen for loading hides. For decades after, cargo went in and out on barges, two or three miles each way.

The only shelter was the estuary at Wilmington, a tidal inlet behind Rattlesnake Island, which is Terminal Island today. Before 1871, by the Army's own measure, the sandbar at the mouth of the estuary had less than two feet of water at low tide. A railroad reached the Wilmington landing in 1869, and in March 1871 Congress put the first two hundred thousand dollars into the harbor there.

So look at the map again. The city of 1850 sits inland, and the water it used was nineteen miles away, in a bay that wrecked ships, and in a shallow estuary. The problem was never just distance. It was water, and the water would have to be made.

<!-- p2 · The jetty, the tide, and the word "never" -->

The first thing that was made was the water itself. In July 1871, the Army approved a plan by Mendell, then a major. It was a jetty, a wall of timber and rock more than a mile long, from Rattlesnake Island to Deadman's Island. It closed the shallows between the islands, so the ebb tide, the water draining out of the estuary, had one channel left. Squeezed into one channel, it dug. By 1881, the bar had ten feet at low tide. By 1888, eleven.

In between came the letter. On November 8, 1886, Colonel Mendell signed it in San Francisco. Wilmington harbor, he wrote, already took ships drawing twelve to fourteen feet, meaning they sat that deep in the water. And still he wrote: "It is a small estuary and can never be made a harbor for deep-water ships." His next sentence set the ceiling: if eighteen or twenty feet at high water could be obtained, the harbor would have reached its maximum development.

So that is what his "never" measured: not the bay, the estuary. In 1897, the federal board that chose the harbor's site would define a deep-water harbor as one for ships drawing at least thirty feet. A ceiling of twenty feet, in a trade that was heading for thirty.

The same letter pointed next door. San Pedro Bay, Mendell wrote, was "worthy of improvement," though he questioned whether an artificial harbor there was worth it, and he asked for a survey. It came back in February 1888 with the first design for a breakwater, a wall out in the bay to block the swell, and Mendell endorsed it. That man, the one who wrote "never," had designed the jetty, backed the breakwater, and would sit on the first board that picked San Pedro.

<!-- p3 · Two bays, three boards, four votes to one -->

Now the map has a choice on it: two bays. In 1890, Congress sent three Army engineers to find the best site for a deep-water harbor on this coast. In December 1891, they answered San Pedro, with Mendell on the board.

The railroad went to the other bay, Santa Monica. In 1891, the Southern Pacific began a pier there, by the account of Charles Willard, a Chamber of Commerce man on San Pedro's side. The spot was called Port Los Angeles. Despite the name, it is not today's port.

A later federal report measured the pier at more than three quarters of a mile, out to about thirty-three feet of water: deep water you could walk to. At a hearing in 1892, the railroad's chief engineer, Hood, called the choice business: anyone who relied on San Pedro alone to move freight from train to ship "would go to the wall." That meant going broke.

In October 1892, a second board of five Army engineers chose San Pedro unanimously. Picking Santa Monica, they wrote, would leave the government keeping "two harbors where one would answer the purpose." The second board had said the same as the first.

It's remembered as the Free Harbor Fight. A free harbor meant one open to competition between railroads, not one free of charge. Willard's side called the railroad's Port Los Angeles a "monopoly harbor"; the one dissenter on a later board would write that spending at San Pedro "would probably eventuate in a serious monopoly." This video doesn't settle that. The board didn't either.

In June 1896, Congress passed a rivers and harbors law. A House bill would have put the money at Santa Monica; the law that passed handed the choice to a board of five, whose majority vote would be final as to the location. On March 1, 1897, that board reported: "this Board reports in favor of San Pedro." Four votes to one. The one, Richard Morgan, filed his minority report two weeks later. So put the deep-water harbor on the map: in San Pedro Bay. And not in Los Angeles. That gap is what the strip of 1906 would cross.

<!-- p4 · A wall in the open sea, in somebody else's town -->

The breakwater came first. On April 26, 1899, San Pedro held a Free Harbor Jubilee. From Washington, President McKinley pressed a button to dump the first barge of rock. The automatic barge failed, and men pushed the stone off by hand. Between fifteen and twenty thousand people watched, according to the Los Angeles Times. The rock came from Santa Catalina Island. In January 1905, with money to spare, the Army got approval to extend the wall to at least nine thousand feet. Stone kept coming, barge after barge, for thirteen years.

Then the second tool arrived. On April 1, 1905, a federal dredge went to work in the inner harbor. What it dug up went behind walls on Terminal Island, the old Rattlesnake Island: made land. By the middle of 1906, the bar had twenty-one feet at low tide. The dredge did what the tide could not.

Now, whose town was that? San Pedro was a city of its own by 1890. Wilmington incorporated as a city in December 1905. Los Angeles, nineteen miles away, had won a harbor it did not touch. Winning the bay was not having it. Look at the outline before the strip: forty-three square miles, well to the north. The harbor sits inside San Pedro and Wilmington. To merge with San Pedro and Wilmington, Los Angeles had to touch them.

<!-- p5 · The law that drew the strip -->

Now the strip itself. It starts with a law, not a road. Since 1883, California had let cities consolidate, that is, merge into one, only if they were contiguous: only if they touched. And its annexation law forbade annexing land that was already a city. So Los Angeles could not swallow San Pedro or Wilmington. It could only merge with them, and only if it touched them.

On the map, that is geometry. Two shapes that don't touch can't merge; a thin bridge of land makes them touch. That bridge is the strip, from the city's southern edge to the boundary lines of San Pedro and Wilmington.

The vote came on Monday, November 12, 1906, in the city and in the territory to be annexed, since the law required a majority in both. The tallies come from the Los Angeles Times, which had campaigned for annexation. Inside the city it was easy, more than seven to one. In the territory, the margin was six votes on election night, and ten in the official count: 213 to 203. It took effect on December 26, 1906.

Now measure it on the city's own annexation map, from 1916. The whole annexation was more than eighteen square miles. But the thin part is less than half of that; the rest is a wide block up north. The thin part is about half a mile wide. On the outline of 1906, it runs about fifteen miles from north to south, all of it across county land: Torrance, Gardena, Lomita and Carson weren't cities yet.

Look at the tip. The legal description follows "the boundary line of said City of San Pedro," and then Wilmington's. It never reached the tide line. So the strip is a legal handshake, drawn half a mile wide, so that one city could touch two others. A handshake is not a merger. That took almost three more years.

<!-- p6 · 1909: the handshake becomes a coast -->

The strip had taken the city to the harbor towns' boundary lines. The towns still had to say yes. In March 1909, a new state law repeated the rule: consolidation only for cities "each one of which is contiguous to the other." That August, each of the three cities voted, and each had to say yes on its own. Wilmington voted on August 4: 107 to 61. Los Angeles said yes the same day, by a landslide. San Pedro, on August 12: 726 to 227.

On August 28, 1909, both consolidations took effect, and Los Angeles, now seventy-six square miles, had a coastline of its own for the first time. The Times, which had campaigned for the strip, put it this way: "Thus Los Angeles becomes in reality a seaport city." Two weeks earlier, Long Beach, the city to the east, had taken in part of Terminal Island by its own vote. From 1909 on, the bay belonged to two cities.

What did Los Angeles get? By a federal report of 1910, about fifteen thousand feet of undeveloped waterfront. And in 1912, the Corps of Engineers called the breakwater finished: just over two miles of rock. The city had owned a coast for almost three years before the wall was done.

<!-- p7 · The seam on today's map, fifty-three feet down -->

Look at the outline today. The city covers about 469 square miles. And the strip is still there, about half a mile wide. On today's outline, the city has grown around both ends, and about nine miles of it still run thin, between other cities and county land.

The harbor at the end of it was deepened by dredges. By 2013, the port and the Corps of Engineers had taken its Main Channel from forty-five feet to fifty-three. Mendell expected eighteen or twenty feet for the estuary. The port's Main Channel is fifty-three.

In 2024, the Port of Los Angeles handled about 10.3 million TEU. A TEU is the space of a single twenty-foot container; a forty-foot box counts as two. By the port's own count, it has been the busiest container port in the Western Hemisphere every year from 2000 through 2024.

So, why is Los Angeles shaped like this? The coast never gave Los Angeles a harbor. Engineers made the water deep. A board of five put the harbor in San Pedro, four votes to one, and not at the railroad's pier. And a city nineteen miles from the harbor towns drew a strip half a mile wide to touch them, then voted itself onto the coast. The strip was never a road to the sea. It was the way to a deep-water harbor that didn't exist yet. The strip is still on the map. So is the water.
