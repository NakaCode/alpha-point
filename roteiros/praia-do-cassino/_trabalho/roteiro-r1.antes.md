This is Praia do Cassino, in Brazil, sold as the world's longest beach. Stone walls mark its two ends, about 132 miles apart in a straight line. The beach itself is less than five miles longer. But how does a coast end up that straight?

Near the middle, it bows out to sea by about 11 miles, a gentle S-curve. Even so, a shore like that should be easy to measure. Yet the state's own tourism website says 158 miles, right next to the words "Guinness Book." In 2025, Guinness World Records told a Brazilian news site it doesn't monitor that title. So why won't the famous number fit on this sand?

You're at Alpha Point, and this is where the story of the beach between two walls begins.

<!-- p1 · Whose word is it? -->

So who decided this was the world's longest beach? Try it yourself. Type "longest beach in the world" into a search box, and one of the top results puts Praia do Cassino at number one. It says the beach has been recognised by Guinness World Records since 1994 as the world's longest sea beach. You'll read the same line in Brazilian newspapers, year after year.

Then, in 2025, the Brazilian news site g1 asked Guinness about it. The answer: Guinness doesn't monitor that title. The fame does have a trail, though. g1 follows it back to a local journalist's history of Rio Grande, the port city at the north end of this sand. In that account, a line about Brazil's longest beach appeared in the Brazilian editions of the record book from 1993 to 1995. Brazil's, not the world's. And it came out after the town to the south objected, which makes a certain sense, because that town owns most of this beach. You'll see how much in a minute. That's as far back as the trail goes in anything we read: three editions of a book, in one country, in the mid-1990s.

Then there's the 158 miles, printed on two pages of the state government's own websites, each with the words "Guinness Book" beside it. Neither page says who measured it, or how. The website that put this beach at number one in your search prints the same 158. It does explain its method: for every beach on its list, it takes the highest length anyone has credibly published. A ranking of published figures, then, more than of sand. As for Guinness, the only beach record we could find on its own website is a different one. Virginia Beach, in the United States, holds it, as the largest pleasure beach.

Other beaches have found their own words. On the Pacific coast of Washington State, an arch at the entrance to Long Beach carries a sign: "World's Longest Beach." That beach runs about 28 miles. Wall to wall, the beach in Brazil is almost five times that long. The city has since switched to a longer title, the world's longest continuous peninsula beach, and says it has no plans to correct the sign. Padre Island, in Texas, is the longest stretch of undeveloped barrier island in the world, in the words of the National Park Service. Cox's Bazar, in Bangladesh, is marketed as the longest unbroken natural sandy beach in the world. Sea beach, pleasure beach, peninsula beach, barrier island: every one of those titles rests on a length. So let's measure this one.

<!-- p2 · Two walls and a ruler -->

So how long is it, really? Stand on the northern wall. It's the breakwater of the Rio Grande seaport, built in the 1910s. It runs out into the Atlantic as a line of stone, right where the Lagoa dos Patos, the big lagoon behind this coast, meets the sea. The beach begins at your feet, against something people built. And it ends the same way, far to the southwest, at another pair of breakwaters, where a small stream called the Chuí reaches the ocean on the border with Uruguay. The world's longest beach, as it's sold, starts and ends at walls people built.

Walk south along the water, and after a while the sand changes its name, with nothing in the sand to mark it. The beach is one strip. What changes is the municipality. The part inside the city of Rio Grande, the part that carries the Cassino name, is about 39 miles. That's how g1 and the state's environmental agency frame it: the city limit is the only line anyone draws. Everything beyond belongs to Santa Vitória do Palmar, the town that objected to the record book. Its tourism secretary has said Cassino can only call itself the longest because it counts the sand all the way to Barra do Chuí, which is theirs. Rio Grande, for its part, says the longest beach begins at Cassino and ends at Barra do Chuí.

Part of that neighbor's share has a new status on the map. In March 2026, Brazil's federal government created the Albardão National Park, and a protected area beside it, on this neighbor's coast. The decree names what it protects: the beach, the dunes, and fossil sites tied to the beach. A senator from Rio Grande do Sul has filed a bill to cancel the decree. He says he doesn't dispute the area's environmental value. His objections are to the form of it: the most restrictive category of protection for most of the area, and a restriction that size. He also questions a public consultation that may not have served its purpose, and points to conflicts with fishing, with offshore wind projects, and with oil and gas in the Pelotas Basin. As of November 2026, the bill was waiting in a Senate committee.

Back to the ruler, then. Three numbers get quoted for this strip, and each has an owner. Brazil's statistics agency maps the shore of the two municipalities at about 135 miles. A national trail network has a route from one set of breakwaters to the other, and puts the walk at 139 miles. That's a distance for walking, not a coastline, and it lands a touch above every coastline we measured. And the state's tourism website says 158.

The famous explanation for the gap is the coastline paradox. It's the one g1 gives, and the encyclopedia, and the ranking website. Measure a coast with a shorter ruler, and you get a longer coast, because the ruler follows wiggles the long one skipped. On a ragged coast, with coves and headlands, the two rulers can disagree by a lot. By that logic, the biggest number is just the most careful one. Let's test it. Put a ruler the size of a town on this shore and step it from wall to wall: you get about 134 miles. Then shrink the ruler to the length of a boat and follow every curve the map has. We did that, on the coastline from OpenStreetMap, and it came out at about 136 miles. We tried the rulers in between, too, and every result fits inside that gap. Less than two percent apart, and nowhere near the state's 158. The paradox is real, in general. On this coast, it barely works.

Then there's the straight line: from wall to wall, it measures about 132 miles, which makes the beach less than five miles longer than the shortest possible path between its own ends. You saw the bow near the middle at the start, the gentle S the map draws. That's the whole of it, and a curve that wide and that slow adds almost nothing to the distance. It isn't ruler-straight, and it barely wiggles. The extra 20-odd miles aren't on the sand, and none of the sources we read says who measured them, or how. So the strange thing about this beach isn't the number. It's the coast.

<!-- p3 · Why the sea draws it straight -->

So what keeps a coast this long this straight? Pull back, until the whole shore of Rio Grande do Sul fits on the screen. From the border with Uruguay to the northern end of the state, it runs about 390 miles, gently curving, with hardly a wiggle in it. Geologists at the state's federal university call it one of the longest barriers of sand in the world. A barrier, because the sea built it: a long bank of sand with lagoons trapped behind it and the ocean out front.

The waves do the drawing. They come in big, mostly from the southeast, onto a coast where the tide is small: well under two feet. So it's the waves, not the tides, that shape this shore, and they get the whole beach to work on, all day, every day. Wherever the shore pokes out, even a little, the waves bend around that point as they come in. The bending concentrates their energy on the bump, and the bump wears down. The sand it loses drifts into the hollows on either side, and the hollows fill. Stand at the water's edge and you can see the trick on a small scale. A crest comes in at a slant, swings around as it feels the bottom, and lands square on the beach. Do that every day, for thousands of years, and the shore loses its bumps. No one drew this coast. The waves did.

And very little comes along to spoil the work. This beach gets almost no new sand from the land. Behind the barrier sit two enormous lagoons, the Lagoa dos Patos and the Lagoa Mirim, more than 5,000 square miles of water between them. The rivers drop their sand there, long before they reach the ocean. In the geologists' count, the barrier opens in only two places along that whole shore. This beach starts at one of them, the channel at Rio Grande, which the freshwater pouring out of the lagoon keeps open. The other opening is far to the north. Between the two walls, by that count, the barrier doesn't open once. That's the word in Cassino's own title, uninterrupted, and this is what stands behind it.

The sand comes from the seafloor instead. The continental shelf off this coast is wide, up to about 120 miles, and shallow, and the sea has been shuffling old sand across it for a very long time. About five thousand years ago, the sea stood several feet higher than its present level, and the shoreline sat farther inland. As the water fell back, the waves pushed sand up into ridge after ridge, each one an old beach, each a little farther out than the one before. Near Cassino, that field of old beaches is up to nine miles wide. The beach you're standing on is simply the newest ridge, the outermost of four generations of barriers stacked along this coast.

The bow near the middle is older than any single wave. It's the shape the high sea left behind, and it follows the seafloor: where the shelf slopes gently, the coast curves inward, and where the shelf drops more steeply, the coast curves out. In the far south, near the border, the sea is doing the opposite of building. Old lagoon mud and peat poke out of the beach there, and the shore is being pushed inland. Different stretches, different work, and none of it adds wiggles. There are no wiggles for the extra miles to hide in. The waves keep ironing them out.

<!-- p4 · What the sand keeps -->

And what does a line of sand this long, this bare, keep? Three things, held along the same straight line, and none of them explains the line. The decree of March 2026 says this sand holds fossil sites tied to the beach, and names them among the things the park is for. What those sites hold, the decree doesn't say, and neither will we. They're part of why the park exists on paper, and part of what the senator's bill would undo.

The second thing is a ship. Since June 1976, a freighter called the Altair has sat on this sand. It didn't drift there. In a storm, as g1 reports, it was run onto the beach on purpose, to keep it from going down, and there it stayed. The same waves have been at it ever since, and the federal university in Rio Grande says that within a few years the ship may be gone.

The third is a date: November 12, 1966. That morning, a total eclipse of the sun was due to cross southern Brazil, and a NASA press release put out ahead of it lays out what was planned. Twenty-one rockets were to go up from a beach about 15 miles south of Rio Grande, near Cassino, fired out over the ocean. Their instruments were to probe the electrified layer of air high above the ground: how dense it was, how hot, and which way the winds blew, before, during and after the eclipse. The rockets were American. The experiments came from scientists of both countries, and Brazil's space commission would run the launches, with American technicians alongside. The release gives one reason for the place, and only one: the beach sat very near the path of the total eclipse. Totality was expected to run a little under two minutes, and the astronauts of Gemini 12 were scheduled to watch it from orbit. How many of those rockets went up, the release can't tell you. It was written before the sky went dark.

Go to the other wall. At the southern end, the Chuí stream slips out to sea between its breakwaters, and across the water is Uruguay. Turn around. Behind you, the sand runs off to the northeast, nearly straight, past the peat and the mud, past the ship, all the way to the wall where the lagoon meets the sea. You can't see that far end from this one. The curve of the earth takes it long before any bend in the beach does. Between the two walls, the waves are doing what they've done since the sea fell back: taking whatever sticks out, and laying it down where the shore falls in. No one drew this coast. The sea did, and it isn't finished.
