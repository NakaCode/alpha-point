This is Praia do Cassino, in Brazil, sold as the world's longest beach. Breakwaters mark its two ends, about 132 miles apart in a straight line. The beach itself is less than five miles longer. But most coasts are a tangle of coves and points. This one looks drawn with a ruler. So who drew it?

Near the middle, in fairness, it bulges out to sea by about 11 miles, in a gentle S. A shore that gentle should be easy to measure. Yet the state's own tourism website says 158 miles, right next to the words "Guinness Book." In 2025, Guinness World Records told a Brazilian news site it doesn't monitor that title. So why won't the famous number fit on this sand?

You're at Alpha Point, and this is where the story of the beach between two walls begins.

<!-- p1 · Whose word is it? -->

Start where most people meet the title: a search box. Try it yourself. Type "longest beach in the world," and one of the top results puts Praia do Cassino at number one. It says the beach has been recognised by Guinness World Records since 1994 as the world's longest sea beach. You'll read the same line in Brazilian newspapers, year after year.

Then, in 2025, the Brazilian news site g1 asked Guinness about it, and got the answer you heard a minute ago. The fame does have a trail, though. g1 follows it back to a local journalist's history of Rio Grande, the port city at the north end of this sand. In that account, a line about Brazil's longest beach appeared in the Brazilian editions of the record book from 1993 to 1995. Brazil's, not the world's. And it was dropped later, after the town to the south objected. That town, as it happens, has most of this beach inside its limits. You'll see how much in a minute. That's as far back as the trail goes in anything we read: three editions of a book, in one country, in the mid-1990s.

The 158 miles, meanwhile, are printed on two pages of the state government's own websites, each with the words "Guinness Book" beside the number. The website that put this beach at number one in your search prints the same 158. It does explain its method: for every beach on its list, it takes the highest length anyone has credibly published. It's a ranking of published figures, then, more than of sand. As for Guinness, the only record for a beach itself that we could find on its own website is a different one. Virginia Beach, in the United States, holds it, as the largest pleasure beach.

Other beaches have found their own words. On the Pacific coast of Washington State, an arch at the entrance to Long Beach carries a sign: "World's Longest Beach." That beach runs about 28 miles. Wall to wall, the beach in Brazil is almost five times that long. The city has since switched to a longer title, the world's longest continuous peninsula beach, and the travel guide Roadside America says there are no plans to correct the sign. In Texas, Padre Island's national seashore is the longest stretch of undeveloped barrier island, says the National Park Service, and Cox's Bazar, in Bangladesh, is marketed as the longest unbroken natural sandy beach. Sea beach, pleasure beach, peninsula beach, barrier island: every one of those titles rests on a length. So let's measure this one.

<!-- p2 · Two walls and a ruler -->

So how long is it, really? Stand on the northern wall. It's the breakwater of the Rio Grande seaport, built in the 1910s. It runs out into the Atlantic as a line of stone, right where the Lagoa dos Patos, the big lagoon behind this coast, meets the sea. The beach begins at your feet, against something people built. And it ends the same way, far to the southwest, at another pair of breakwaters, where a small stream called the Chuí reaches the ocean on the border with Uruguay. The world's longest beach, as it's sold, starts and ends at walls people built.

Walk south along the water, and after a while the sand changes its name. The beach is one strip. What changes is the municipality. The part inside the city of Rio Grande, the part that carries the Cassino name, is about 39 miles. That's how g1 and the state's environmental agency frame it: the city limit is the only line anyone draws. Everything beyond, more than two-thirds of the sand, belongs to Santa Vitória do Palmar, the town that objected to the record book. Its tourism secretary has said Cassino can only call itself the longest because it counts the sand all the way down to the border, and that sand is theirs. Rio Grande, for its part, says the longest beach begins at Cassino and ends at Barra do Chuí. Same sand, in other words. The argument is over the name on it.

Back to the ruler, then. Every careful measurement we found lands within a few miles of every other. Brazil's statistics agency maps the shore of the two municipalities, and its line is one of them. A national trail network lists a walking route from one pair of breakwaters to the other, and the route is a touch longer. Our own rulers, which you're about to see, fit in the same few miles. Only one number sits out on its own. The state's tourism website says 158.

The famous explanation for the gap is the coastline paradox. It's the one g1 gives, and the encyclopedia, and the ranking website. Measure a coast with a shorter ruler, and you get a longer coast, because the short ruler follows wiggles the long one skipped. On a ragged coast, with coves and headlands, the two rulers can disagree by a lot. By that logic, the biggest number is just the most careful one. Let's test it. Take a ruler 30 miles long. Lay one end on the northern breakwater, and swing the other end down the coast until it touches sand again, and mark the spot. Pick the ruler up, set it on the mark, and swing it again. Each step skips whatever the shore does in between: a bulge, a dip, a bend. By the time the ruler touches the breakwater at the Chuí, you've taken only a handful of steps, and you have your number. Then shrink the ruler to the length of a boat, and follow every curve the map has, thousands of steps, in and out of every bend. We did that, on the coastline from OpenStreetMap, and the two answers land less than two percent apart. We tried the rulers in between, too, and every result fits inside that gap. Nowhere near the state's 158. The paradox is real, in general. On this coast, it barely works.

Then there's the straight line: from wall to wall, it measures about 132 miles, and the longest answer any ruler gave us was about 136. Less than five miles, between the shortest possible path and the long way round every curve. You saw the bow near the middle at the start, the gentle S the map draws. That's all there is to it, and a curve that wide and that slow adds almost nothing to the distance. It isn't ruler-straight, and it barely wiggles. The extra 20-odd miles aren't on the sand, and none of the sources we read says who measured them, or how. So the strange thing about this beach isn't the number. It's the coast.

<!-- p3 · Why the sea draws it straight -->

So what keeps a coast this long this straight? Pull back, until the whole shore of Rio Grande do Sul fits on the screen. From the border with Uruguay to the northern end of the state, it runs about 390 miles, gently curving, with hardly a wiggle in it. Geologists at the federal university of Rio Grande do Sul call it one of the longest barriers of sand in the world. A barrier, because the sea built it: a long bank of sand with lagoons trapped behind it and the ocean out front.

The waves do the drawing. They come in big, mostly from the southeast, onto a coast where the tide is small: well under two feet. So it's the waves, not the tides, that shape this shore, and they get the whole beach to work on, all day, every day. Picture one place where the shore pokes out, even a little. A wave coming in meets that point before it meets the sand on either side. As it feels the bottom, it slows there, and the crest bends around the point, the way a line of marchers wheels around a corner. The bending gathers the wave's energy onto the bump, and the bump wears down. The sand it loses drifts into the hollows on either side, and the hollows fill. The sea isn't so much taking that sand away as moving it over, from where the shore sticks out to where it falls in. Stand at the water's edge and you can see the trick on a small scale. A crest comes in at a slant, swings around as it feels the bottom, and lands square on the beach. Do that every day, for thousands of years, and the shore loses its bumps. Think of it as ironing. Every pass takes a little of the wrinkle out, and the sea doesn't put the iron down. No one drew this coast. The waves did.

And very little comes along to spoil the work. This beach gets almost no new sand from the land. Behind the barrier sit two enormous lagoons, the Lagoa dos Patos and the Lagoa Mirim, more than 5,000 square miles of water between them. The rivers drop their sand there, long before they reach the ocean. In the geologists' count, the barrier opens in only two places along that whole shore. This beach starts at one of them, the channel at Rio Grande, which the freshwater pouring out of the lagoon keeps open. The other opening is far to the north. Between the two walls, by that count, the barrier doesn't open once. A beach can't be the longest unless it's one beach, and that is what stands behind this one.

The sand is old sand instead, worked over by the sea again and again. Out front, the continental shelf is wide, up to about 120 miles, and shallow, and it has a part to play. About five thousand years ago, the sea stood several feet higher than its present level, and the shoreline sat farther inland. As the water fell back, the waves pushed sand up into ridge after ridge, each one an old beach, each a little farther out than the one before. Near Cassino, that field of old beaches is up to nine miles wide. The beach you're standing on is simply the newest ridge, the outermost of four generations of barriers stacked along this coast.

The bow near the middle is older than any single wave. It's the shape the high sea left behind, and it follows the seafloor. Where the shelf slopes gently, the coast curves in. Where it drops more steeply, the coast curves out. That bow is the "almost" in the title. In the far south, near the border, the sea is doing the opposite of building. Old lagoon mud and peat poke out of the beach there, and the shore is being pushed inland. Different stretches, different work, and none of it adds wiggles. There are no wiggles for the extra miles to hide in. The waves keep ironing them out.

<!-- p4 · What the sand keeps -->

Some of what a line of sand this long, this bare, keeps is written down. In March 2026, Brazil's federal government created the Albardão National Park, and a protected area beside it, on the southern town's stretch of this coast. Among what the decree protects: the beach, the dunes, and fossil sites tied to the beach. A senator from Rio Grande do Sul has filed a bill to cancel the decree. He says he doesn't dispute the area's environmental value. His objection is to the form of it: the most restrictive category of protection for most of the area, and a restriction that size. He also questions whether the public consultation did its job, and points to conflicts with fishing, with offshore wind, and with oil and gas. As of November 2026, the bill was waiting in a Senate committee.

Two more things sit on the sand itself. One of them is a ship. Since June 1976, a freighter called the Altair has sat on this sand. It didn't drift there. In a storm, as g1 reports, it was run onto the beach on purpose, to keep it from going down, and there it stayed. The same waves have been at it ever since, and the federal university in the city of Rio Grande says that within a few years the ship may be gone.

The other is a date: November 12, 1966. That morning, a total eclipse of the sun was due to cross southern Brazil, and a NASA press release put out ahead of it lays out a plan for this sand. Twenty-one rockets were to go up from a beach about 15 miles south of Rio Grande, near Cassino, and fly out over the ocean. Their instruments were to probe the electrified layer of air high above the ground, before, during and after the eclipse. The release gives one reason for the place: the beach sat very near the path of the total eclipse. Overhead, the astronauts of Gemini 12 were scheduled to watch it from orbit. How many of those rockets went up, the release can't tell you. It was written before the sky went dark.

Go to the other wall. At the southern end, the Chuí stream slips out to sea between its breakwaters, and across the water is Uruguay. Turn around. Behind you, the sand runs off to the northeast, nearly straight, past the peat and the mud, past the ship, all the way to the wall where the lagoon meets the sea. You can't see that far end from this one. The curve of the earth takes it long before any bend in the beach does. Between the two walls, the waves are doing what they've done since the sea fell back: taking whatever sticks out, and laying it down where the shore falls in. No one drew this coast. The sea did, and it isn't finished.
