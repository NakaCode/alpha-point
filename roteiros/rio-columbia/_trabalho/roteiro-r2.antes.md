Every year, before the spring snowmelt, part of the Arrow Lakes Reservoir, on the Columbia River in British Columbia, is emptied on purpose. And starting with the 2025 flood season, the United States pays Canada for that empty space: 37.6 million dollars a year, adjusted for inflation.

But this river is mostly American: only about 15 percent of the land it drains lies in Canada. A much bigger share of its water comes down through British Columbia.

The payment is temporary: part of a deal that runs out after the spring of 2027, unless both countries agree on something new. Talks to update the treaty between them have been paused since March 2025.

And the strange part: that treaty already promises Canada's help in a big flood, if the United States asks. So why pay for empty space, when the United States can already call on Canada in a flood?

You're at Alpha Point, and this is where the story of the lake that waits for the flood begins.

<!-- p1 · The river that starts up north -->

Put your finger on the map where the Columbia River begins. You may know its American end: this is the largest hydropower-producing river system in the United States. Its beginning is somewhere else. It's a lake in the mountains of British Columbia, called Columbia Lake.

From there, the river does something odd for a river on its way to Oregon. It heads northwest, deeper into Canada. Then it swings around in a long hairpin, the river's big bend in British Columbia, and only after that does it turn south toward the border. That bend is underwater these days: it lies beneath a lake, behind one of the dams in this story. Keep your finger moving, and by the time the Columbia crosses into the United States, it has made a third or more of its whole journey to the sea.

So where does all that water come from, and when? The Canadian part of the basin, the land that drains into the river, is mountain country, and what lands on it in winter is snow. For months, the snow just sits there. Then, late in the spring, it lets go. About 60 percent of the water that runs off the whole basin in a year comes down in May, June and July. Most of the river's year arrives in one season.

A large part of it arrives by way of Canada. You can read that off two gauges. A gauge is just a station on the bank that records how much water is going past. One of them sits in British Columbia, a short way north of the border. The other is far downstream at The Dalles, a place on the river in Oregon where the United States government measures it. We lined the two records up, year against year, for the 87 years they share. On average, the river at the Canadian gauge is already carrying about 39 percent of the water that will pass The Dalles. That's our own calculation, from the two governments' data. About 15 percent of the land, and about 39 percent of the water.

Numbers like those make you wonder who holds the tap. Hold that thought; we'll measure it. BC Hydro, the power company that runs Canada's side of the treaty, describes the river this way. Most of the power, and most of the need to hold back floods, is in the United States. The big sites for storing water are in Canada. One country has the need. The other has the room.

Before the big storage dams, the river at that Canadian gauge carried about ten times more water in June than in February. A river like that floods. And in 1948, it did.

<!-- p2 · The flood and the bargain -->

That spring, the river came up over its banks, and the damage ran from Trail, in British Columbia, all the way to Vanport, a town on the edge of Portland, Oregon. At Vanport, a railroad embankment was doing the work of a dike. It broke on a Sunday afternoon, and within an hour, by the US Geological Survey's account, the town was completely destroyed. Across the whole flood, the same report counts at least 51 people dead, and tens of thousands out of their homes.

What do you do with a river that arrives all at once? You catch part of it upstream, and you let it down slowly. That takes room: a deep valley, a dam across it, and a long lake with space left to rise. On the Columbia, the big sites for that were north of the border.

The early ideas pulled in opposite directions. A few years after the flood, the United States proposed a dam of its own whose lake would back up into Canada. Canada answered, by the US Congressional Research Service's account, with a plan to divert much of the upper Columbia for its own use.

Out of that argument came a treaty. It took effect in September 1964, and the two countries' leaders marked the day at the border itself. Canada would build three big storage dams. The United States would build the one it had asked for, Libby. Its lake backs 42 miles into Canada, and by the treaty's own count, the water it regulates makes extra power there too.

Then follow the money, because it runs the other way. Water comes down, and money goes up. For the room behind the Canadian dams, the United States paid 64.4 million dollars in lump sums, as each dam came into service, for 60 years of flood protection. The price was half of what those dams were expected to save it in flood damage. Most of the money was for one lake: Arrow, the lake at the start of this story. So paying Canada for room is as old as the treaty itself. The strange part starts when those 60 years run out.

The other half of the bargain is power. Water stored in Canada and released on a schedule lets the American dams downstream make more electricity than they could on their own. The treaty splits that gain down the middle: half of the extra power is Canada's share, and it arrives as electricity, not cash. British Columbia sold the first 30 years of that share up front, for about 254 million dollars, and that money, with the flood payment, helped build the three dams. So American money helped build Canada's dams, and water stored in Canada helped run America's.

For 60 years, that was the bargain: room for money, and stored water for power. Year after year, the snow melted, and the lakes caught part of it.

<!-- p3 · The trigger and the tap -->

Maybe you've already decided what the payment is. It's insurance. The United States bought decades of cover, the cover ran out, and so it pays by the year instead. That's close, and it leaves out the strange part. In September 2024, the 60 years the United States had paid for ran out. The promise didn't. Under the treaty, Canada has to help in a big flood whenever the United States asks, and the United States covers what that help costs. That's what it means to have Canada on call.

And the treaty doesn't say how big a flood that is. No number, no line on a gauge. A flood on this river is measured in cubic feet per second: how much water goes past The Dalles every second. When the treaty came up for review, the two sides worked from different numbers. American planners projected that Canadian storage would come into play once the river's peak landed somewhere between 450,000 and 650,000. The Canadian side assumed it would be called only at 600,000, a flood expected to be rare. That's how the US Congressional Research Service describes those studies. Between 450,000 and 600,000, one side expects the call and the other doesn't. We'll call that the gap.

So we looked at the river itself. From 1879 to 1967, the yearly peak at The Dalles passed the Canadian side's line in 40 of 89 years. On the old river, that was no rare flood. Since 2009, with the space reserved upstream, it hasn't passed it once, and six springs have peaked in the gap: high enough for the American range, too low for the Canadian line.

That gap is where the law comes in. In January 2025, Congress authorized the payment. It pays for reserving 3.6 million acre-feet of room a year behind the dam that holds back the Arrow Lakes. An acre-foot is enough water to cover an acre of land one foot deep. The law spells out what that includes, in its own words: "operations that the Government of Canada may not be obligated to provide" under the treaty.

The lake drops every winter anyway, as water moves downstream to make power. What the United States pays for is a guarantee: that much room, every year of the deal, whatever the treaty does or doesn't require.

The alternative has a price too. The American agencies that run the treaty once estimated that a call on Canada would cost between 4 and 34 million dollars per request. British Columbia's estimate is that relying on those calls would cost the United States 40 to 150 million dollars a year in lost power. Beside the American figure, which is per call, 37.6 million a year looks steep. Beside the Canadian one, which is per year, it looks cheap.

So yes, the United States can call on Canada in a flood. And no number says when. Under the deal, it pays so the room is already there, call or no call. The treaty is the promise. The deal is the room. What the United States is buying is certainty.

Certainty about what, exactly? Not about whether Canada could shut the river off. As a tariff fight between the two countries grew, CBC News asked whether ending this treaty could help Canada fight back. In March 2025, British Columbia's energy minister addressed the idea. "Canadian action to terminate the treaty would have little effect on the current dispute," he said. For one thing, leaving takes ten years' written notice, and nothing we've read says either side has given it.

For another, look at what the dams can hold. Together, the three treaty lakes in Canada can store as much water as the whole river carries past The Dalles in about six weeks. Measured against the river as it runs through British Columbia, near the border, that's closer to three and a half months. Then the lakes are full, and the rest of the river keeps coming. So Canada can't turn the Columbia off. What it can do, under plans agreed with the United States, is decide when a big share of that water comes down.

Remember that Canadian gauge? Before the big storage dams went in upstream, on both sides of the border, June dwarfed February there. With all of them in place, the river's busiest month carries only about twice its quietest, and the total for the year has barely changed. The water wasn't taken away. It was moved: into winter, to make power, and out of spring, to leave room for the flood.

And the timing isn't Canada's alone to set. Canada did not turn over control of its reservoirs to the United States, BC Hydro says, and the two sides have to agree on how the treaty lakes are run.

What Canada has in its hands, then, is a calendar. And for every spring of the deal, the United States pays to have one page of it settled in advance.

<!-- p4 · What the room was before -->

That room came from somewhere. Stand on the shore of the Arrow Lakes in late winter, when the water is low, and it's in front of you. There's a wide strip of bare ground between the lake and the trees. By summer, the water will cover it. Part of that strip is the room, seen from the ground. Before the treaty dams, people lived along these valleys.

The government of British Columbia puts the count at about 2,300 people displaced, and more than a dozen small communities flooded. Some, like Edgewood and Burton, were relocated. Renata no longer exists. Farther north, the river's big bend in British Columbia went under another of the treaty's lakes, and a town went under with it. As for how residents and First Nations were consulted, the province's own words are that it "could be considered inadequate to non-existent by today's standards."

The Arrow Lakes lie in the traditional territory of the Sinixt. In 1956, the government of Canada declared the Arrow Lakes Band extinct. Decades later, the Supreme Court of Canada wrote those words in a ruling. It found that a successor group of the Sinixt, based in Washington State, is an Aboriginal people of Canada.

And the river had lost something even before the treaty dams. Grand Coulee Dam, built by the United States far downstream and finished in 1941, stopped the natural migratory runs of fish, salmon among them, on the upper Columbia.

<!-- p5 · What each side puts on the scale -->

So that is the room, and what it cost. What it's worth depends on who is counting.

As the paid years ended, each side gave something up. Canada agreed to a smaller share of the power. The cut was more than a third. The United States agreed to pay by the year for room it used to have paid up.

Each side has its own account of what the other gains. The American agencies that run the treaty once estimated that ending it would raise American power revenues, mostly by ending Canada's share. British Columbia disputed those assumptions. Its argument is that the treaty makes the river dependable, and that a dependable river is worth more to the United States than the share it sends north. Two careful sums, and they don't land in the same place.

When you hear that the United States pays, it's fair to ask who that is. The payment for the room is federal: Congress authorized it. Canada's share is power, and part of the bill for that power falls on utilities along the river. Three of them, in Washington State, went to court and stopped paying their part, and in September 2026 they settled with the federal government.

There is a longer plan, in outline. Shortly before the paid years ended, the two governments announced an agreement in principle on a modernized treaty. It has not been finalized. In March 2025, British Columbia's government announced that the United States had paused the talks. Canada's delegation to them includes representatives of three First Nations. As of August 2026, the magazine Canadian Geographic reported that the talks had not resumed.

That leaves the clock. The temporary deal can cover up to three flood seasons, and the spring of 2027 is the third. The law that authorizes the payment expires at the end of August 2027, unless Congress extends it. A Senate bill would move that date to 2029. The treaty itself remains in force, on-call promise, missing number and all.

It starts with snow. By the end of winter, the mountains above the Arrow Lakes are holding a large part of the year's water, and none of it has moved. Down in the valley, the lake is at its lowest point of the year, with that strip of bare ground showing. Nothing much is happening yet. The room is there, and the flood isn't.

Then the melt comes. Through May and June, the creeks fill, the river rises, and the lake climbs back up its shore until the bare ground is gone. Part of a flood that would have reached Portland stays up in the valley instead, behind a dam in British Columbia, and goes down later, a little at a time.

Next winter, the lake will drop again, and the snow will come back. For now, the United States pays to have that room waiting. And the spring of 2027 is as far as this deal can reach.
