This is Balboa, the town below Balboa Heights, the hillside the Panama Canal Zone was run from. In nineteen sixty, about half of its three thousand one hundred thirty-nine residents had been born in the United States, and, under a nineteen oh-three treaty, its police, courts and schools answered to Washington. Until October first, nineteen seventy-nine. But being born in Balboa didn't, by itself, make you an American.

Balboa was one town in the Zone, a band ten miles wide that the United States ran across Panama, from the Caribbean to the Pacific. Up the canal, Paraíso had seven residents born in the United States, out of three thousand one hundred thirteen. On the Atlantic side, Rainbow City had three, out of three thousand six hundred eighty-eight. And from nineteen twenty to nineteen seventy, no census of the Zone published how many of its residents were citizens. It counted where they were born. So what made Balboa American, if being born there didn't? You're at Alpha Point, and this is where the story of who was American in the Canal Zone begins.

The first rule was written in Washington, fifteen days after Panama broke from Colombia. On November eighteenth, nineteen oh-three, John Hay signed a treaty for the United States, and Philippe Bunau-Varilla signed it acting for Panama. Its second article granted the United States, "in perpetuity," a band ten miles wide, five on each side of the planned canal, to build, run and protect it. Panama City, the capital, and Colón sat inside those limits, and the same article left them out.

Its third article gave the United States, inside that band, "all the rights, power and authority" it "would possess and exercise if it were the sovereign of the territory." On that band, American law then set up a government of its own. A nineteen twelve act of Congress named it the Canal Zone and gave it a governor, appointed by the president. It also gave it a federal court, with juries drawn "from among the citizens of the United States." A nineteen fourteen executive order put the police, the schools and the post office under the Zone's government.

The nineteen twelve act also let the president declare every acre of the Zone necessary to the canal. By June nineteen fifteen, almost four thousand privately owned houses had been destroyed or removed. The canal's nineteen fourteen report calls Balboa's ground "the permanent gold site at Balboa," and La Boca, beside it, "the permanent silver settlement." In nineteen fourteen the administration moved into a new building up on Balboa Heights. The town you saw first is Balboa, the town at the foot of Balboa Heights, the hillside the Canal Zone was run from.

In September nineteen fourteen, a boundary convention fixed "the permanent boundary line between the City of Panama and the Canal Zone," monument by monument. From now on, that is the line. When Balboa was planned as a permanent town, the plan already had two words in it: "gold" and "silver."

Those two words were payrolls. The Zone's government called them the "gold roll" and the "silver roll." Its own nineteen forty report explains the names: during construction, common laborers had been paid in silver coin, and skilled craftsmen, recruited largely from the United States, had been paid in gold. By then everyone was paid in dollars, and the names stayed, on pay, on housing, on the commissary, the Zone's own store, and on the clubhouses.

In nineteen twenty and nineteen thirty, the Zone's reports describe the "silver roll" as its West Indian workforce. In nineteen forty, the "gold roll" was, in the report's words, "with a few exceptions, citizens of the United States," and the "silver roll" held "a considerable number" of Panamanians.

The division ran through the map. The Zone's nineteen twenty report lists "gold" clubhouses at Balboa and four other towns, and "silver" clubhouses at La Boca and four others. Gatún and Cristóbal had both. It lists "white" schools and "colored" schools, and prints what it paid their teachers on average: in the first, one hundred fifty-eight dollars and forty-six cents a month; in the second, seventy-six dollars.

In nineteen thirty, more than sixty percent of the Zone's "silver" employees lived outside it, in Panama City and Colón. By nineteen forty, the Zone's free schools took their children only if the family lived inside. Balboa, the "gold site," and La Boca, the "silver settlement," sat pressed together at the Pacific entrance, with Panama City across the line. So the Zone sorted its workers by payroll. The next rule decided who could live inside the line at all.

The rule came by treaty: signed in nineteen thirty-six, in force from nineteen thirty-nine. From then on, the Zone had a short list of who could live inside it. People who worked for the United States government, the canal or the railroad, with their families. Members of the armed forces of the United States, with their families. Contractors, a few licensed businesses, church, charity, school and science workers, and everyone's household servants. The test was service, not citizenship. Nobody lived in the Zone just because they wanted to.

The house came from the canal. The Zone's nineteen fifty report says its employees on "U.S." rates had to be assigned family quarters before their families got permission to come at all. Mary and Herbert Knapp taught in the Zone's schools. They wrote in the Panama Canal Review, the Zone's own magazine, in its issue dated the day the Zone ended: everyone "lived in rented quarters" and shopped at the "commy," the commissary. The job came first. Then the house. Then the family.

Many of the others inside the line were soldiers. In nineteen seventy, the last American census of the Zone counted birthplaces, not citizens. More than half of its residents had been born in the United States, and the count took in the bases along with the towns. So the number for the whole Zone says less than it seems. Now go back to Balboa. More than a thousand of its three thousand one hundred thirty-nine residents in nineteen sixty had been born in the Zone itself. So what was a child born in Balboa, in the eyes of American law?

The answer is one sentence of law, and it was written in nineteen thirty-seven. Being born in the Zone didn't, by itself, make you a citizen of the United States. Under that law, you were one if your father or mother was a citizen of the United States at the time of your birth. Congress enacted it on August fourth, nineteen thirty-seven, and made it reach back to February twenty-sixth, nineteen oh-four, the day the nineteen oh-three treaty took effect.

Until nineteen thirty-seven, the State Department's manual says, a child born in the Zone fell under the general rule for births abroad. That rule, the manual adds, required the citizen parent to have lived in the United States first, a condition the nineteen thirty-seven law dropped, back to nineteen oh-four. And the same manual says something the treaty never did. For citizenship law, it says, the Zone "was never considered part of the United States." The same country held, inside that Zone, every power it would have "if it were the sovereign."

So for the thousand or so residents of Balboa born in the Zone, the answer came from their parents, not from where they were born. The sorting was done elsewhere: on the payroll. And in nineteen forty-six, Washington asked about it.

In February nineteen forty-six, the State Department sent its embassy in Panama an instruction "concerning racial discrimination in the Canal Zone." The Zone's governor answered the ambassador. The "silver" workers, he said, wanted "the complete abolition of any segregation in the Canal Zone." Granting that soon, he added, would wreck the canal and railroad organizations. The Zone's own nineteen forty report had called the division "a matter of long custom in tropical countries."

The names came off the pay tables in November nineteen forty-eight, and off the signs in public places by December. By nineteen fifty, the Zone's reports sort pay into "U.S." rates and "local" rates instead. In nineteen fifty-five, a new treaty promised the same base pay for the same job, whether the employee was a citizen of the United States or of Panama. A nineteen fifty-eight law set it.

The store sorted its customers, too. From nineteen thirty-nine, the Zone's commissaries sold only to its employees and soldiers, their families, and certain residents. From the end of nineteen fifty-six, employees who lived in Panama and were not citizens of the United States could no longer shop there. A State Department memo says Panama had insisted on that, calling the old access unfair competition with its economy.

Then there were the schools. In nineteen twenty, the Zone's report listed "white" schools and "colored" schools. By the nineteen sixties, its reports listed "U.S. citizen schools" and "Latin American schools." Now the nineteen sixty census, town by town. Balboa, at the Pacific end: about half of three thousand one hundred thirty-nine born in the United States. Paraíso, up the canal: seven of three thousand one hundred thirteen. Rainbow City, on the Atlantic side: three of three thousand six hundred eighty-eight. In the nineteen fifty census, it was called Silver City. The payroll had lost its old names. The division was still there. And the next fight was about a flag.

From nineteen sixty, Panama's flag began to fly inside the Zone, in places chosen one by one. It flew first at Shaler Triangle, a plaza of Zone ground in Panama City, then on the bridge over the canal and on the Administration Buildings. Under a January nineteen sixty-three agreement between the two governments, both flags were to fly wherever the Zone's civilian authorities flew the American one. On December thirtieth, nineteen sixty-three, the Zone's governor took the American flag down everywhere outside the chosen sites. At the schools, it would fly only indoors.

On January seventh, nineteen sixty-four, students at Balboa High School hoisted the American flag on their own, "in defiance of the Governor's orders," as a State Department bulletin put it. Two days later, on the afternoon of January ninth, students from Panama's National Institute walked to the school carrying their own flag.

An international commission of jurists, called in by Panama's bar association, pieced the next hours together. The police let a delegation of five walk the flag toward the pole, there was shoving, and the Panamanian flag tore. By seven that evening the students had left the Zone along Fourth of July Avenue, the street beside the line in front of the National Institute. Four days of fighting had begun.

Four U.S. soldiers and twenty-four Panamanians were killed, according to the U.S. National Archives. Other counts differ. On January tenth, Panama broke off relations with the United States. The question of who belonged had moved from the payroll to the flagpole. The nineteen seventy-seven treaty opens "in the spirit of the Joint Declaration of April third, nineteen sixty-four," the one that restored relations after those four days. That treaty is where the Zone ends.

On September seventh, nineteen seventy-seven, in Washington, President Carter and Panama's Omar Torrijos signed two treaties. In April nineteen seventy-eight, the Senate approved the canal treaty, sixty-eight to thirty-two. It took effect on October first, nineteen seventy-nine. Its first article "terminates and supersedes" the nineteen oh-three treaty, which had granted the band "in perpetuity" to build, run and protect the canal. That day, seventy-five years after the United States took possession of the canal works, Panama took back full jurisdiction over the former Zone.

The Canal Zone and its government ceased to exist. At Albrook Air Force Base, Vice President Mondale called it "a proud day" for both peoples. In August, the Secretary of State had written to him that the visit should reassure the American canal employees that the United States "is not abandoning them." For thirty months more, American police and courts still covered the canal's American employees, the military and their families, in certain areas. The Zone's schools passed to the Department of Defense, and an American agency, the Panama Canal Commission, ran the canal until the end of nineteen ninety-nine.

So what made Balboa American, if being born there didn't? A treaty, which granted the United States "all the rights, power and authority" within a band ten miles wide. A law, which gave the Zone a governor and seated its juries with citizens of the United States. A second treaty, which let only those who worked or served there, and their families, live inside the line. And a payroll, which sorted the people who worked for the canal: first into "gold" and "silver," then into "U.S." rates and "local" rates. Being born there, by itself, was never enough.

The treaties said the rest in their own words. In nineteen oh-three, those were the powers the United States would have "if it were the sovereign." In nineteen seventy-seven, the new treaty called Panama the "territorial sovereign." The Panama Canal Authority, in twenty twenty-six, gives its address as the Administration Building, in Balboa. The town stayed where it was, below the hillside. The Zone that made it American did not.
